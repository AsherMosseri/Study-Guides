Density

  • Population density: people vs. land, as if spread evenly.
  • Arithmetic = people ÷ all land. U.S.: 326 million, 86 per sq mi (33 per sq km).
  • Bangladesh 2,962 · Netherlands 1,068 · Japan 869 per sq mi.
  • Misleads: hides empty Alaska and the West, and Egypt's desert.
  • Physiologic = people ÷ arable land.
  • Egypt: 97.6 million; 252 → 6,995 per sq mi; 98% of people on 3% of the land (Nile valley and delta).
  • Switzerland: physiologic = 10 × arithmetic (1 acre in 10 arable).
  • Ukraine: 44.8 million; 192 → 281 (× 1.46: 1 of every 1.46 acres arable).
  • Agricultural (AP) = farmers ÷ farmland: rural Bangladesh very high, Iowa very low.
  • Middle America and the Caribbean high; South America moderate; India lowest in South Asia and has more farmland per person than China.

Population data

  • U.S. census every 10 years: money and seats in Congress depend on it.
  • Undercounted: migrants, racial minorities, doubled-up or homeless families, low-income families.
  • Debates: sampling vs. counting everyone; the citizenship question.
  • UN, World Bank, Population Reference Bureau: numbers don't always match; often estimates.

Distribution and clusters

  • Distribution: the pattern people make across space. Clustered = close together; dispersed = few, spread out.
  • People lived near food; farm technology and shipping loosened that link.
  • Dot map (Fig. 2.4: 1 dot = 100,000 people). Dasymetric map (Fig. 2.5) uses land cover and terrain.
  • China + India = ⅓ of humanity (empty Himalayas, China's desert).
  1. East Asia: almost ¼ of humanity, 1.4 billion in China; Huang He and Yangtze ribbons; wheat and rice.
  2. South Asia: 1.5 billion; India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka; Ganges, Indus, Brahmaputra; hemmed in by Himalayas, Afghan/Pakistani mountains, Indian Ocean. India 1.17 vs. China 0.56; India most populous by 2030 (1 in 6).
  3. Europe: 725+ million; dense even in mountains; Industrial Revolution first; urban: Germany 77%, UK 83%, France 80%.
  4. North America: megalopolis Boston–Washington, D.C. and Quebec City–Windsor; 70+ million; half of Canada; 20% of Americans; ¼ the size of Europe's cluster.
  • Big three ≈ 4 billion of 7.7 billion. South America + Africa barely beat India.
  • Dhaka 122,700 · Mumbai 68,400 · NYC 28,717 per sq mi. Rural Bangladesh 3,000–5,000 vs. rural Iowa 55.

Opening and big ideas

  • Shanghai: 128-story tower (2,000+ ft); ~22 million; China 1.4 billion, 100+ cities over 1 million.
  • Coal → PM-2.5 (reaches lungs and blood). North China lives 5.5 years less. Fixes: limit coal, ban imported plastics for burning, limit cars.
  • Demography = study of population trends.
  • Scale: downtown → suburbs vanishes on a world map; national averages hide states.
  • Yangon: density + poverty + weak infrastructure = vulnerability; 2008 cyclone, Irrawaddy delta, ~100,000 dead.
  • Luxor: environment shapes where people live, not just culture.

Malthus and his critics

  • Ehrlich, The Population Bomb (late 1960s): population outpacing food.
  • Malthus, 1798: food grows linearly, population exponentially; revised 1803–1826; assumed a country eats what it grows.
  • Wrong because: countries aren't closed systems (globalization; mercantilism, colonialism, capitalism); crops spread (potato → Ireland, 1700s); Norway 2% arable, imports food; yields rose (seeds, pesticides, fertilizers, irrigation, GMOs).
  • Neo-Malthusians: focus on population; limits of food, energy and water; farming's environmental costs.

Growth around the world

  • CBR/CDR = births/deaths per 1,000 per year. NIR leaves out migration. Negative: Europe and Russia.
  • Soviet growth fell after 1991. India nearly 3% thirty years before the book; now Africa 2.43% vs. India 1.17%; HIV/AIDS.
  • Palestinian territories 2.83 · Afghanistan 2.65 · Sudan 2.55 · Yemen 2.52. Iran, Oman, Morocco dropping.
  • Limited women's opportunities + men running family life → high growth.
  • India below world avg. but above China; Japan shrinking; Thailand negative; Indonesia and Vietnam slowing.
  • South America a bit over 1%; Brazil 2.9% → 0.8%; Argentina, Chile, Uruguay low.
  • Lowest: global economic core (U.S., Canada, Europe, Japan, Australia, Uruguay).
  • Russia after 1991: health, alcoholism, drugs, male suicide, economy. Ukraine negative.
  • More urban → lower natural increase.

Fertility and aging

  • TFR: average children per woman 15–49. 95+ countries (41% of people) below 2.1.
  • 2016: world 2.4 (the book also says 2.43), Niger 7.2, South Korea 1.2. UN: below 2.2 by 2050.
  • Drops: Kenya 3.85; China 6.1 → 1.75 in 35 yrs, 1.5 (2010), 1.62 (2016); Iran 6.8 (1980) → 1.7 (2016).
  • Old-age dependency: Europe 29.9 → 47 by 2050; sub-Saharan Africa 5.7. Child dependency: Africa 74 vs. Europe 23.
  • Aging = pensions and care, fewer young taxpayers. Fixes: raise TFR or immigration.
  • Japan: 128.06M (2008) → 126.8M (2017) → ~100M (2050, −20%); discourages immigration; 98%+ Japanese.
  • Raising births: Sweden, Russia (leave, daycare); Japan (men's housework). Limited success.
  • Still growing because of India, Indonesia, Bangladesh, Pakistan, Nigeria.

India

  • 29 states + 7 union territories. North grows fast; south and west slow (female literacy, land ownership, health care, birth control).
  • 1952: first population program in the world.
  • 1976: forced sterilization of men with 3+ children; quotas; Maharashtra 3.7 million; riots; 6 million men in all.
  • Now: states set policy (Maharashtra pays newlyweds to wait 2 years); birth control for women, injectables, posters, village clinics.
  • Contraceptive prevalence (women 15–49): India 55.1%; Andhra Pradesh 69.8, Maharashtra 63.5, Bihar 26.0, Meghalaya 21.1.
  • Lesson: a world map hides what's inside a country.

Religion and growth

  • Catholic Church and some conservative Islam oppose contraception. Northern Nigeria grows faster than the south.
  • But the Catholic heartland grows slowly; the Philippines grows fast.
  • Bordeaux (de Blij, from Dakar): "Where are all the children?" Europe is aging.
  • Saudi Arabia ~2%; Indonesia's program from 1970 (coercion + inducement) → 1.6% (2000), 1.1% (2016).

Demographic transition model

StageBirthsDeathsGrowthExamples and causes
1HighHigh, swingingLowMost of history. Black Death, 1300s: Crimea → Sicily → north on rats' fleas (contagious diffusion); killed ¼–½; Britain ~4M → ~2M. Famine and war.
2HighFalling fastVery highEurope after ~1750: food, sanitation, soap, medicine. Death rate ~35 (Europe, before 1750) → ~16 (Britain, 1850). Today: Afghanistan, Palestine (vaccines).
3Falling fastFallingLow, still growingBritain ~1870 through the world wars: women's opportunities, later marriage, fewer child deaths. Brazil (contraceptives); Mali (schooled girls have ~half as many children).

Based on western Europe after the Industrial Revolution. Late-1980s World Bank SPL predictions (U.S. 276M by 2035, Brazil 353M by 2070, Mexico 254M by 2075, China 1.4B by 2090, India 1.6B by 2150) were unrealistic: China passed 1.2B in 1994, India 1B in 1998. Newer: China 1.45B in 2030, India 1.7B in 2060.

Common traps

  • Rates are per 1,000: divide by 10 for a percent (25 per 1,000 = 2.5%, not 25%).
  • Stage 2 vs. 3: if births have started falling, it's stage 3.
  • Natural increase leaves out migration: a negative NIR country can still grow through immigration.
  • Arithmetic vs. physiologic: pressure on farmland is physiologic.
  • ¼ vs. ⅓: East Asia is ~¼ of humanity; China + India together are ⅓.
  • 55 vs. 86: rural Iowa is 55 per sq mi; the U.S. average is 86.
  • 3% vs. 2%: Egypt's people live on 3% of the land; Norway's land is 2% arable.
  • 1952 vs. 1976: India's first program vs. the sterilization drive.
  • 2.4 vs. 2.43: the book gives both for the 2016 world TFR.
  • Over 40%: the text says ages 0–14 are over 40% in most lower-income countries; use that, not the chart.
  • Religion isn't destiny: the Catholic heartland grows slowly.
