3.3 Explain the Theories of Migration and Understand the Motivations for Migration pp. 77–82

Section 3.1 described the kinds of movement and 3.2 the migrations people were forced into. This section asks the question underneath all of them: why do people choose to move? Researchers have been working on it for more than a century.

  1. How alike the source and the destination are.
  2. How well information flows back from the destination to the source.
  3. How far apart the source and the destination are.

Laws of migration and the gravity model pp. 77–78

More than 100 years ago, a British demographer named tried to answer why people move by choice. He studied records of and came up with several "laws of migration." Many still hold today. Among them:

Ravenstein based his laws on moves within England, yet they fit flows across the world. When a question describes a flow, check it against the five laws: is it short, is the long move aimed at a big city, is it rural people and young adults doing the moving, and is anyone moving back?

Ravenstein also thought the size of a migration flow follows , an idea from Chapter 1: the farther something travels from its origin (its hearth), in distance or in time, the less likely it is to spread there (Fig. 3.16). For migration, that means the farther away a destination is, the fewer migrants reach it. Lots of people move short distances; few move long ones.

Ravenstein's observation about volume was an early version of the . The gravity model is a math formula that predicts how much two places will interact, and how likely people are to migrate between them (or send goods, calls or money), from just two things: how many people each place has and how far apart they are.

  • Interaction (such as migration) rises as the places get bigger and more important.
  • Interaction falls as the distance between them grows.

Still, more migrants make short moves than long ones even now, so the model remains somewhat useful.

That's also how an intervening opportunity works: a nearer place can beat a bigger, farther one.

Push and pull factors p. 78

The gravity model gives a rough expectation, but people don't migrate by formula. Real decisions weigh two kinds of reasons:

  • are the conditions and perceptions that help a person decide to leave a place: work or retirement conditions, the cost of living, personal safety and security, environmental disasters and hazards, even weather and climate.
  • are what draws a person to a particular destination and help them decide where to go.

A decision to emigrate from home and immigrate somewhere new comes from a mix of pushes and pulls.

A migrant knows home firsthand, but often knows the destination only from what they've heard and read. Some migrants picture their destination as better than it really is, and that rosy image can be a powerful pull.

Because interaction with faraway places drops off with distance, people feel much less sure about distant destinations than nearby ones. So many migrants end up stopping closer to home than they first planned, when an comes along: a chance near where the migrant is now that makes moving on to the farther place much less attractive.

first to a village, then to a nearby town, later to a city, and finally to a huge metropolis like São Paulo or Rio de Janeiro. Each step brings new pull factors. At any step, the family may decide that what they've found is an intervening opportunity: a better reason to stay than an uncertain future at the place they were aiming for.

Types of push and pull factors p. 78

Migrants weigh whether to leave, and where to go, using what they've lived through at home and what they believe about the place they're headed. The reading groups the factors into five types: legal status, gender, ethnicity and race, environmental conditions, political conflict, and family links.

Climate change.

  • One report predicts 150 to 300 million climate refugees from 40 countries by 2050.
  • Pacific countries, from tiny Tuvalu (12,000 people) to huge Australia (24.1 million), already face freshwater shortages, more wildfires in drought-stricken areas, and stronger storms.

Political conflict pp. 81–82

(more in 3.4). People may fear their culture and traditions won't survive a big political change, and move somewhere nearby where more people share them and they can be safe.

The partition of South Asia, 1947. When it left, it split the colony into two countries:

  • a Hindu-majority India, and
  • a Muslim-majority Pakistan in two pieces 800 miles apart: a western part (today's Pakistan) and an eastern part (today's Bangladesh).
  1. The British drew the new borders in secret.
  2. They held independence ceremonies for Pakistan on August 14, 1947 and India on August 15, 1947.
  3. Only after both countries were independent did the British reveal the borders and maps.
  4. Fear and uncertainty took over. On August 17, a mass migration of 14 million people began (Fig. 3.22):
    • 7 million Muslims on the Indian side moved to the new, Muslim-majority Pakistan.
    • 7 million Hindus and Sikhs on the Pakistani side moved to Hindu-majority India.

Partition and the migration that followed left a painful memory for generations of South Asians, and started a split between India and Pakistan that is still unresolved.

Family links and chain migration p. 82

When migrants decide where to go, they're drawn to places where family and friends have already done well.

  1. A migrant picks a destination and moves.
  2. They text, call or pass word home, telling family and friends about the new place. That paints a good picture of it, and they may offer help finding housing and a job.
  3. Once the migrant can say a community has formed there, a place where newcomers will feel at home, more people follow the same path.

3.4 Identify Why Refugees Are a Distinct Group of Migrants and Describe Where Most Refugees Migrate pp. 83–90

Refugees, asylum and repatriation p. 83

are migrants running from political persecution and violence at home. They travel on foot or by boat, carrying little.

The 1951 Refugee Convention is the international law that spells out who counts as a refugee and what rights refugees have.

  • The (the Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees) helped most of Europe's refugees return home after the war.

The UNHCR, the International Red Cross and the World Food Program work with other groups and governments, especially in the countries refugees flee to, to keep refugees safe and bring relief. The UNHCR organizes and pays for international relief and negotiates with governments and political groups for refugees.

It protects them because their own governments are the ones threatening their basic human rights.

  • the right to be protected and to stay, for a time, in a country other than their own.
  • The UNHCR, Red Cross and World Food Program give refugees food and shelter, usually in refugee camps, in the countries that granted them asylum.

The UN works to make sure refugees aren't forced back while persecution is still going on. Once the violence and persecution ease and conditions improve, the UNHCR helps refugees go home, a process called .

  • Accepting them means agreeing that each one has the legal right to live and work in the U.S.
  • Each refugee is screened (vetted) and gets help from a nongovernmental organization to find housing and settle in.

have also fled persecution and human rights abuses at home and are asking another country for protection, but they haven't yet been legally recognized as refugees.

  1. They arrive at a port or land border of the country where they want asylum and make their claim there.
  2. Countries usually hold asylum seekers in a holding facility until the claim is decided.
  3. They meet a judge or government official, explain the persecution they faced, and give evidence for their claim.
  4. Cases take time. Meanwhile, some asylum seekers are kept in detention centers and others live in temporary housing.
  5. The government decides whether to grant asylum, which gives the asylum seeker refugee status. If it says no, it can send them back home.

North Africa and Southwest Asia pp. 87–88

Iraq. The Gulf War (1991) and the Iraq War (2003) each produced millions of refugees.

  • They abandoned their villages and towns and streamed toward and across the Turkish and Iranian borders: as many as 1.85 million people.
  • The U.S. and its allies set up a secure zone for Kurds in northern Iraq, hoping displaced Kurds in Turkey and Iran would come home. It only partly worked, and many are still refugees in Turkey and Iran.
  • The 2003 Iraq War produced over 2 million refugees, most living in neighboring Syria and Jordan, plus 2.8 million IDPs.
  • About 200,000 Syrian Kurds became refugees in Iraq, where a 1971 Iraqi law gives refugees the legal right to stay and grants them protection.
  • Iraq also hosts many Iranian refugees from the Iran–Iraq conflict more than 30 years ago, including members of the People's Mujahedin of Iran (MEK), an opposition group exiled from Iran in 1980.

Afghanistan.

  2. 1980s: the Soviets expected a quick win, but their generals underestimated the Afghan resistance. U.S. weapons for the Afghan rebels helped force a stalemate and then a Soviet withdrawal (1989), followed by a power struggle among Afghan factions. Most of the more than 6 million refugees in Iran and Pakistan, about one-quarter of Afghanistan's people, stayed where they were.

  4. 2001: Afghanistan became a base for anti-Western terrorists, leading up to the attacks on the U.S. on September 11, 2001. Before the expected military response began, tens of thousands of Afghans poured across the borders even though Pakistan and Iran tried to close them.

The 1800s: open doors, with one exception.

  • Apart from that, the U.S. kept its doors open to immigrants through the 1800s.
  • Most came from northern Europe (Scandinavia) and western Europe (Ireland, Great Britain, Germany and France).

After World War I: quotas.

  • U.S. politics turned toward , a policy of staying out of other countries' affairs.
  • Because most immigrants living in the U.S. in 1910 had come from northern and western Europe, the quotas let in far more people from there than from southern and eastern Europe (Fig. 3.31).
  • , lowering the yearly total to 150,000 and discouraging eastern and southern Europeans even more. Figure 3.30 shows the steep drop.
  • That effectively shut out large-scale immigration from Asia.
  • With these laws in place and the Depression underway, immigration slowed to a trickle in the 1930s.

After 1940: loosening, labor and refugees.

  • From 1942 until the program ended in 1965, Mexican migrants went mainly to the American Southwest and Florida.
  • 1943: Congress gave China the same status as European countries. 1952: Japan got similar status.
  • Immigration began rising again (Fig. 3.30).
  • Estimates vary, but more than 7 million migrants may have entered as refugees between 1945 and 1970.

1965: the quota system ends.

  • The new limits: 170,000 a year from countries outside the Western Hemisphere and 120,000 from countries in the Americas (Fig. 3.30).
  • Mexicans now crossed into the U.S. and stayed instead of going back and forth (Fig. 3.32).
  • Refugee and guest worker policies in the following decades let many more migrants in than those limits would suggest.

1986 and after.

Post–September 11 pp. 92–93

Before 9/11, the Border Patrol focused mainly on drug trafficking and human smuggling. The new policies affect asylum seekers and both authorized and unauthorized migrants.

  • On March 25, 2003, Human Rights Watch criticized the policy for treating people as suspected terrorists based only on their nationality.
  • On April 17, 2003, the Department of Homeland Security quietly ended Operation Liberty Shield.

Europe.

  • The U.S. took a "hard" approach, expanding immigration controls.
  • Since people move freely among most European countries, European governments worked to keep unauthorized migrants out of that larger shared space, while promoting policies to get migrants to buy in to their new society.
  • Austria's integration package includes 300 hours of language training and a civics course, which is often hard on newly arrived migrants who depend on social welfare support.

Whether or not that's true, worry about a link between migration and terrorism will keep shaping security policy in the U.S., Europe and elsewhere for a long time.

Policies change migration in three ways: they block it (quotas, walls, bans), invite it (Bracero, guest workers, wet feet, dry feet), or create it (new borders at Partition and in Sudan). Notice how often the result wasn't what the government meant: the border fences didn't stop crossings, they moved them into the desert.
