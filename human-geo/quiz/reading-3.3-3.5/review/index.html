Models and formulas

Gravity model
population A × population B ÷ distance bigger places interact more; farther apart, less. The AP course often squares the distance; the book doesn't
Distance decay
the farther the destination, the fewer migrants more short moves than long ones (Fig. 3.16)
Flow strength
how alike the places are + how well information flows back + distance
Displaced, 2018
40M IDPs + 25.4M refugees + 3.1M asylum seekers the book calls it 8.6% of the world; the math gives under 1%

Ravenstein's laws

  1. Every flow makes a countermigration.
  2. Most migrants move a short distance.
  3. Long-distance migrants pick big cities.
  4. Rural people migrate more than urban.
  5. Young adults move abroad more than families.
  • British demographer; studied internal migration in England, 100+ years ago.
  • His law on volume (distance decay) → the gravity model. Planes and the Internet weaken it, but short moves still win.

Push and pull

  • Push: leave. Work, retirement, cost of living, safety, disasters, weather and climate.
  • Pull: go here. Often vaguer: based on perception, sometimes too rosy.
  • Intervening opportunity: a nearer chance makes the farther goal less attractive.
  • Rural Brazil: village → town → city → São Paulo or Rio (AP: step migration).
  • Any factor can push or pull. Five types: legal status; gender, ethnicity, race; environment; political conflict; family links.

Legal status

  • Authorized (documented): has a visa. Unauthorized: visa expired, or crossed without permission.
  • U.S.: 43.7M migrants, 10.7M unauthorized (peak 12.2M before the recession); 2/3 of unauthorized adults here 10+ years.
  • Mexico 26%; South and East Asia 27%. U.S. TFR 1.8: immigration keeps it growing, adds workers and taxpayers.
  • Temporary visas: doctors, engineers, construction, hotel, restaurants. 45,000+ seasonal farmworkers; Canada since 1966; mostly Mexico and Central America.
  • Fig. 3.18 (2000–2017): Mexico 2,897,000; Dominican Rep. 688,000; Cuba 637,000; El Salvador 403,000.
  • Border: fences, agents, tech; crossers pushed east into the desert; coyotes (smugglers); bottles and memorials (Tijuana, Fig. 3.19).
  • Goods move freely, people don't. Slowdown: more the economy than walls.

Gender, ethnicity and race

  • Jobs and safety depend on them; hiring by them shows power and reinforces stereotypes.
  • Well-off women hire helpers of another ethnicity.
  • Toronto agencies (Stiell and England): Caribbean "docile" → "aggressive"; Filipinas "naturally docile."

Where refugees go

  • 83% stay in their region; low-income → low-income neighbors.
  • 2018 sources: Syria 6.3M, Afghanistan 2.6M, South Sudan 2.4M. Hosts: Turkey 3.5M, Pakistan 1.4M, Uganda 1.4M.
  • Totals: 2.9M (1970, mostly Palestinians) → 8M+ (1980) → 25.4M (2018), incl. 5.4M Palestinians in camps.
  • 60% of UNHCR's refugees: Afghanistan, Syria, Sudan, South Sudan, Somalia, DR Congo.

Environment

  • Earthquakes, hurricanes, volcanoes, tsunamis, floods, fires, climate change; often temporary.
  • Katrina (2005): wetlands destroyed by flood control, extraction, Army Corps, oil, developers. 85%+ flooded; population −11% (2000–2010); children 27% → 23%; families to outer suburbs (Fig. 3.20).
  • Climate refugees: 150–300M from 40 countries by 2050.
  • Tuvalu: 12,000 people; atolls 2 m (6.5 ft) up. Australia 24.1M. Tuvaluans reject "climate refugee": passive victims; lets rich emitters off; migration a last resort (Farbotko).

Politics and family

  • War makes refugees; new borders make migration too.
  • Partition, 1947: British rule 1857–1947. Hindu India, Muslim Pakistan (two parts, 800 mi apart). Borders secret until after independence (Pakistan Aug 14, India Aug 15). From Aug 17: 14M moved (7M Muslims → Pakistan; 7M Hindus and Sikhs → India); ~1M died. Punjab split.
  • Chain migration: family and friends follow the same path; chains → immigration waves.

Refugees and asylum

  • Refugee: fled persecution or violence, crossed a border; most walk to a neighbor.
  • 1951 Convention (for postwar Europe): well-founded fear of persecution for race, religion, nationality, social group, political opinion.
  • UNHCR + Red Cross + World Food Program: relief, camps, negotiation.
  • Asylum (UN Declaration of Human Rights): protection, temporary stay.
  • Repatriation: going home when safe. Resettlement: a third country, permanently (U.S.: 160,000 Karen from Burma via Thai camps, 2007–2017).
  • Asylum seeker: claim at a port or border; held; judge decides; refugee status or sent back.
  • IDP: displaced but still inside their country; not refugees; often uncounted; can be worse off.

Areas of dislocation

  • Syria (2011): 5.6M abroad (Turkey, Lebanon, Jordan, Iraq); 6.6M IDPs. Zaatari: 3 sq mi, 100,000+. Arsal: 50–80,000, town ×3.
  • Rwanda (mid-1990s): Hutu vs. Tutsi; 800,000–1M killed; 2M+ to Zaire and Tanzania; 500,000 home after 1996.
  • Israel/Palestine: diaspora ~70 CE; Zionism; <50,000 Jews (1910); Balfour 1917; mandate 1919–48; 750,000 by 1948; 600,000 Palestinians out (1948); 1967 settlements ("facts on the ground"); 2M Soviet Jews (1991); 7.8M people. Jordan: permanent refugees; Lebanon: temporary.
  • Afghanistan: ¼ of world's refugees. 1979 Soviets → double stream (Iran 2.5M, Pakistan 3.7M); 6M+ stayed; Taliban 1996 → counter-migration.
  • Iraq: 1.85M Kurds (1991); 2003: 2M+ refugees, 2.8M IDPs; 200,000 Syrian Kurds; 1971 law.

More dislocation

  • West Africa: 1.5M (1997, Liberia, Sierra Leone) → <270,000 (2013).
  • Sudan: colonial borders; war 1983–2005 (2.2M dead, 5M displaced, 1.6M to Uganda); oil 1999; Darfur: Muslim vs. Muslim, 400,000+ killed, 2.5M displaced; Powell: genocide (2004). South Sudan 2011 → new refugees.
  • DR Congo: ~6M dead since 1998; LRA 1M+ (2009).
  • South Asia 3rd. Sri Lanka: Tamils vs. Sinhalese; 90,000 uprooted. Bangladesh: 156.6M, ≤20 ft, 230 rivers; pumping, mangroves; 17% by 2050; India's wall.
  • SE Asia: 1–2M fled Vietnam (1975); Khmer Rouge 1.7M killed; Myanmar IDPs.
  • Europe: Yugoslavia, 6,056,600 (1995). Colombia 5.7M IDPs. Haiti 2010: 200,000 dead.

Policies block, invite or create

  • Block: quotas, Chinese exclusion, White Australia, U.S. fences, India's wall, Liberty Shield.
  • Invite: Bracero, temporary visas, refugee admissions, resettlement.
  • Create: new borders: Partition (1947), Israel (1948), South Sudan (2011).
  • Side effects: fences moved crossers into the desert; Bracero's end turned cyclical moves into permanent ones.

Common traps

  • Refugee vs. IDP: the border decides. IDPs aren't refugees under the 1951 Convention.
  • Refugee vs. asylum seeker: recognized vs. still waiting.
  • Unauthorized includes visa overstays, not only border crossers.
  • Intervening opportunity vs. chain migration: a nearer chance vs. following family.
  • Repatriation vs. resettlement: home vs. a new third country.
  • 1921 vs. 1924: 3% of 1910 vs. 2% of 1890.
  • Darfur was Muslim against Muslim: not only religion.
