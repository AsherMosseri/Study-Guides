Laws of migration and the gravity model pp. 77–78

  1. Studies of voluntary migration say the strength of a migration flow depends on what three things?

    How alike the source and destination are, how well information flows back from the destination, and the distance between them.

  2. Who was Ernst Ravenstein, and what data did he study?

    A British demographer who, more than a century ago, studied records of internal migration in England and proposed "laws of migration."

  3. What does Ravenstein's first law say every migration flow produces?

    A return flow, or countermigration.

  4. According to Ravenstein, how far do most migrants move?

    A short distance.

  5. According to Ravenstein, where do migrants who move long distances tend to go?

    To big cities.

  6. According to Ravenstein, who migrates more: urban or rural people?

    Rural people. City residents are less migratory.

  7. According to Ravenstein, who is more likely to move to another country: families or young adults?

    Young adults. Families are less likely to make international moves.

  8. What is distance decay, and what does it predict for migration?

    The farther something gets from its origin (hearth), in distance or time, the less likely it spreads there. For migration: the farther the destination, the fewer migrants reach it.

  9. What is the gravity model?

    A math prediction of how much two places interact (including migration) based on their population sizes and the distance between them. Interaction rises with size and falls with distance.

  10. How do you calculate the gravity model, according to the book?

    Multiply the populations of the two places, then divide by the distance between them.

  11. Which of Ravenstein's ideas was an early version of the gravity model?

    His law on volume: that migration flows shrink with distance (distance decay).

  12. Why does the gravity model mean less today, and why is it still somewhat useful?

    Airplanes and the Internet changed what distance means. But more migrants still move short distances than long ones.

Push and pull factors p. 78

  1. What are push factors? Give the reading's examples.

    Conditions and perceptions that help a person decide to leave: work or retirement conditions, cost of living, personal safety and security, environmental disasters and hazards, even weather and climate.

  2. What are pull factors?

    What attracts a migrant to a particular destination; the factors that help them decide where to go.

  3. What does a decision to emigrate and immigrate come from?

    A combination of push and pull factors.

  4. Why are pull factors often vaguer than push factors?

    They may rest only on perceptions from things heard and read, not experience. Migrants can have unrealistically positive images of a destination, which makes a strong pull.

  5. What is an intervening opportunity?

    An opportunity near the migrant's current location that makes moving to a farther destination much less attractive, so they stop closer to home.

  6. Why do many migrants end up closer to home than they planned?

    Interaction drops with distance, so they feel less certain about faraway places, and an intervening opportunity nearby can win out.

  7. How would a family from rural Brazil likely move, following Ravenstein's laws?

    In steps: village → nearby town → city → metropolis (São Paulo or Rio de Janeiro). At any step an intervening opportunity may make them stay.

  8. What AP term describes moving toward a destination in stages, like the rural Brazil family?

    Step migration. (The book describes it but doesn't use the term.)

  9. Can one factor be both a push and a pull? What are the five types the reading covers?

    Yes, any single factor can push or pull. The types: legal status; gender, ethnicity and race; environmental conditions; political conflict; family links.

Legal status pp. 78–79

  1. What makes a migrant authorized (documented)?

    A visa from the host country, giving the formal right to live and work there for the visa's length, usually months or years.

  2. What are the two ways someone becomes an unauthorized (undocumented) migrant?

    Entering legally with a visa and staying after it expires, or crossing a border without permission.

  3. How does immigration help the U.S. population, according to the reading?

    It gives positive net migration that supplements a TFR of only 1.8, keeping the population growing, and it provides a labor supply and tax base.

  4. How many migrants live in the U.S., and how many are unauthorized?

    About 43.7 million; 10.7 million unauthorized, the lowest since the prerecession spike of 12.2 million.

  5. What shares of all U.S. migrants come from Mexico and from South and East Asia?

    Mexico 26%; South Asia and East Asia 27%.

  6. How long have most unauthorized adult migrants lived in the U.S.?

    Two-thirds have lived there more than 10 years.

  7. What does Fig. 3.17 show about foreign-born residents, 1990–2012?

    The biggest increases were along the coasts; a band of northern counties from Idaho to Maine saw declines.

  8. What kinds of jobs do U.S. temporary work visas fill?

    Many kinds: doctors, computer engineers, construction workers, hotel cleaners, restaurant waitstaff, plus seasonal farm and forestry work.

  9. How many farmworkers enter the U.S. on seasonal papers, and where do U.S. and Canadian farmworkers mostly come from?

    Over 45,000 a year; mostly from Mexico and Central America. Canada has run a similar program since 1966.

  10. In Fig. 3.18, which country sent the most authorized immigrants to the U.S. in 2000–2017, and about how many?

    Mexico, about 2.9 million (2,897,000). Next: Dominican Republic (688,000) and Cuba (637,000).

  11. How has U.S. spending on the Mexican border changed the border region?

    Fences, more Border Patrol agents and technology have changed the region's cultural landscape. The fences are hard to climb but have openings where people can talk across.

  12. What effect did new fences and security south of San Diego have?

    They pushed people crossing without documents farther east into the desert.

  13. What are coyotes?

    Smugglers who take people across the border for a large fee.

  14. What marks the border fences, and what does Fig. 3.19 show?

    Empty water bottles and memorials to Mexicans who died crossing. Fig. 3.19: the double wall between Tijuana and San Diego, with crosses placed by human rights activists.

  15. What does the reading say about globalization and the free flow of people? Why has unauthorized migration slowed?

    Goods flow more freely, but the free flow of people is far from realized. The slowdown may have more to do with the economy than with walls and fences.

Gender, ethnicity and race pp. 79–80

  1. What do a migrant's job and safety opportunities depend on, and what does hiring by these traits do?

    Gender, ethnicity and race. Hiring by them makes power relationships visible and reinforces stereotypes about each group's role.

  2. Why do some high-status women hire domestic helpers from a different ethnic group?

    To set themselves apart from the people who work for them. The helper's ethnicity signals who holds power in the home and region.

  3. What did Stiell and England find about placement agencies in Toronto?

    They used scripted stereotypes: Caribbean workers first "docile," cheerful and good with children, later difficult, "aggressive" and selfish; Filipinas "naturally docile," hardworking and willing to work long hours without complaint.

Environmental conditions pp. 80–81

  1. What environmental crises cause migration, and are those flows always permanent?

    Earthquakes, hurricanes, volcanic eruptions, tsunamis, floods, fires and climate change. They can be temporary: many people return after the crisis.

  2. How did people make Hurricane Katrina's damage worse?

    Flood-control projects, resource extraction, the Army Corps of Engineers, oil companies and developers destroyed Louisiana's wetlands, sped erosion, and brought the Gulf to New Orleans's doorstep.

  3. How much of New Orleans flooded, and how much did its population fall between 2000 and 2010?

    More than 85% flooded; population fell 11% (Katrina plus the 2008 recession).

  4. How did the share of children in New Orleans change after Katrina, and where did families go?

    From 27% (2000) to 23% (2007). Families with children moved to farther-out suburbs.

  5. Which New Orleans neighborhoods lost the most children in Fig. 3.20, and with what effect?

    Mid-City, Gentilly, the Garden District and the Ninth Ward: fewer schools needed and changed neighborhoods.

  6. How is climate change expected to affect migration?

    More erratic weather will drive migration as people use it as an adaptive response.

  7. How many climate refugees does one report predict, from how many countries, by when?

    150 to 300 million from 40 countries by 2050.

  8. What climate effects are Pacific countries from Tuvalu to Australia already facing?

    Freshwater shortages, more wildfires in drought areas, and stronger storms. (Tuvalu: 12,000 people; Australia: 24.1 million.)

  9. What is Tuvalu, and why is it at risk?

    A small Pacific country of atolls barely 2 m (6.5 ft) above sea level, threatened by climate change that starts with emissions from high-income countries far away. Migrating is one adaptation.

  10. Why do Tuvaluans reject the label "climate refugee," according to Carol Farbotko?

    It paints them as passive victims and ignores industrialized countries' responsibility to cut emissions. They see migrating abroad as a last resort.

  11. What did Farbotko say media and environmental groups were doing with Tuvalu?

    Portraying it as a place in crisis whose only option is to leave as refugees, pushing aside real Tuvaluan voices and speaking for them to convince climate skeptics.

Political conflict pp. 81–82

  1. How do political conflict and borders cause migration?

    War and conflict create refugee flows, and changing or creating borders also generates migration: people who fear for their culture move where people like them are safe.

  2. How did Britain partition South Asia in 1947?

    Into a Hindu-majority India and a Muslim-majority Pakistan in two parts 800 miles apart (today's Pakistan and Bangladesh). It drew the borders in secret and revealed them only after independence.

  3. When did Britain rule South Asia, and when did Pakistan and India become independent?

    Ruled 1857–1947. Pakistan: August 14, 1947; India: August 15, 1947. Mass migration began August 17.

  4. How many people moved during Partition, who went where, and how many died?

    14 million: 7 million Muslims to Pakistan, 7 million Hindus and Sikhs to India. About 1 million died.

  5. What lasting effects did Partition have, and what did the border do to Punjab?

    A traumatic memory for generations and an India–Pakistan split that still festers. It cut Punjab, the Sikh homeland, between the two countries.

Family links and chain migration p. 82

  1. What is chain migration?

    Migration along kinship (family and friendship) links: early migrants send word home and help with housing and jobs, and others follow the same path.

  2. How do early migrants encourage others to follow?

    By calling, texting and passing word home that creates a positive picture of the place, promising help with housing and jobs, and reassuring them a community has formed.

  3. What do chains of migration build into?

    Immigration waves connecting the home country to the destination.

Refugees, asylum and repatriation p. 83

  1. Who are refugees, and where do most go?

    Migrants fleeing political persecution and violence at home. Most walk to a neighboring country.

  2. What is the 1951 Refugee Convention, and what was its main goal?

    International law defining who is a refugee and their legal rights. Its main goal: helping European refugees after World War II.

  3. What is the 1951 Convention's definition of a refugee?

    A person with a well-founded fear of persecution because of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

  4. What is the UNHCR, and which other groups help refugees?

    The Office of the UN High Commissioner for Refugees: it organizes and funds relief and negotiates for refugees. The International Red Cross and World Food Program also help.

  5. Why do refugees have legal status under international law?

    To protect them, because their own governments are threatening their basic human rights.

  6. What is asylum, and what guarantees refugees the right to it?

    The right to be protected and to stay temporarily in a country other than their own. Guaranteed by the UN Declaration of Human Rights.

  7. Where do refugees usually get food and shelter, and why do some stay for generations?

    In refugee camps in the countries giving asylum. Where instability lasts, families live in camps for generations.

  8. What is repatriation, and when does it happen?

    Refugees returning home, helped by the UNHCR, once violence and persecution decrease. The UN works to make sure refugees aren't forced back while persecution continues.

  9. What is resettlement? Give the reading's example.

    A country other than the one giving asylum welcomes refugees permanently. The U.S. took 160,000 Karen refugees from Burma (Myanmar) living in camps in Thailand, 2007–2017.

  10. What happens to each refugee the U.S. resettles?

    They get the legal right to live and work in the U.S., are vetted, and get help from a nongovernmental organization with housing and adjusting.

  11. What is an asylum seeker?

    Someone who has fled persecution and asks another country for protection but has not yet been legally recognized as a refugee.

  12. What is the asylum process?

    Claim asylum at a port or land border → held in a facility, detention center or temporary housing → explain the persecution to a judge or official with evidence → government grants asylum (refugee status) or can send them home.

Distribution of refugees pp. 83–85

  1. What share of refugees flee to a country in their own region, according to the UNHCR?

    83%. The countries producing and receiving the most refugees are near each other.

  2. Which countries produced the most refugees in 2018?

    Syria (6.3 million), Afghanistan (2.6 million), South Sudan (2.4 million).

  3. Which countries received the most refugees in 2018?

    Turkey (3.5 million), Pakistan (1.4 million), Uganda (1.4 million), next door to the top source countries.

  4. How do most refugees travel, and between what kinds of countries?

    On foot, by bicycle, wagon, open boat or caravan, with few resources. Mostly from low-income countries to neighboring low-income countries, so the poorest countries feel the impact most.

  5. How many refugees were there in 1970 and 1980, and who were most of them in 1970?

    2.9 million in 1970, mostly Palestinian Arabs displaced by Israel's creation and the wars that followed. Over 8 million in 1980 (nearly triple).

  6. How many refugees did the UNHCR count in 2018, and how many were Palestinian Arabs in camps?

    25.4 million, including 5.4 million Palestinian Arabs who have lived in camps since 1970.

  7. What does Fig. 3.24 map, and which places stand out?

    The average number of refugees from each country of origin, 2001–2018. Places with major political conflict, like Syria and Afghanistan, stand out.

  8. What is the difference between a refugee and an internally displaced person (IDP)?

    A refugee has crossed an international border and is in another country. An IDP left home but stays inside their own country. Under the 1951 Convention, IDPs are not refugees.

  9. Why can IDPs' lives be even more unstable than refugees', and why are they often uncounted?

    They stay in the country where the crisis is happening (violence, flooding, famine). They're uncounted because they don't cross borders.

  10. How many IDPs, refugees and asylum seekers were there in 2018, and what share of the world does the book say was displaced?

    40 million IDPs, 25.4 million refugees, 3.1 million asylum seekers. The book says 8.6% of the world. (The math gives under 1%; use 8.6% for the book's figure.)

Areas of dislocation: Syria and Rwanda pp. 85–86

  1. Which regions generate more than half of all refugees, and which six countries account for about 60% of UNHCR's refugees?

    North Africa and Southwest Asia and sub-Saharan Africa. The six: Afghanistan, Syria, Sudan, South Sudan, Somalia, the DR Congo.

  2. How many Syrians fled abroad after the civil war began, when did it begin, and where did they go?

    5.6 million since 2011, to Turkey, Lebanon, Jordan and Iraq, which already had large refugee populations or conflicts of their own.

  3. What is Zaatari?

    A temporary UNHCR camp in Jordan near the Syrian border, only 3 sq mi (7.8 sq km), that became a more permanent home for over 100,000 refugees in harsh, cramped conditions.

  4. What happened in Arsal, Lebanon?

    50,000–80,000 Syrian refugees arrived and now outnumber locals; its power, waste and water systems strain to serve almost three times its original population. (ISIS controlled it 2014–2017.)

  5. How many Syrians are internally displaced?

    6.6 million.

  6. What happened in Rwanda in the mid-1990s?

    A civil war: militant Hutu against the minority Tutsi and "moderate" Hutu. 800,000 to 1 million killed; more than 2 million fled to Zaire (DR Congo) and Tanzania; the strife spread to Burundi.

  7. What happened after Rwanda's civil war calmed in 1996?

    The UNHCR and the World Health Organization helped 500,000 Rwandans repatriate.

Israel/Palestine pp. 86–87

  1. What started the Jewish diaspora, and when?

    Persecution under the Roman Empire, around 70 CE, scattering Jews to Europe.

  2. Why did the land become sacred to Muslims, and how did people live there from 700 to the 1900s?

    About 600 years later, Muhammad ascended to heaven beside Judaism's holiest site. Muslims, Christians and Jews then lived in relative peace, except during the Crusades.

  3. What is Zionism, and why did it grow in the late 1800s?

    The movement for a national homeland for Jews between the Mediterranean and the Jordan River. It grew because of rising persecution of Jews in Europe, the roots of the Holocaust (6 million killed).

  4. How many Jews lived in the region in 1910, and how many had migrated by 1948?

    Fewer than 50,000 in 1910; as many as 750,000 had migrated by 1948.

  5. What was the Balfour Declaration, and when was Palestine a British mandate?

    A 1917 letter from a British official to a prominent Jewish family promising a Jewish homeland in Palestine. Britain ran Palestine as a League of Nations mandate 1919–1948.

  6. What did the UN decide for the Palestinian mandate, and what happened when the 1948 borders were drawn?

    To divide it into Palestine and Israel. About 600,000 Palestinian Arabs fled or were pushed out, becoming refugees in Jordan, Egypt, Syria and beyond.

  7. How do Palestinian refugees' situations differ in Jordan and Lebanon?

    In Jordan, they're so integrated they're considered permanent refugees. In Lebanon, they wait in camps and still count as temporary refugees.

  8. What did Israel do after the 1967 Six-Day War?

    Expanded its territorial control and built settlements for Jewish immigrants in Palestinian territories, which Murphy calls "facts on the ground" on strategic, defensible sites.

  9. What migration followed the Soviet Union's breakup, and what is Israel's population?

    2 million Jews moved from the former Soviet Union to Israel. Israel: 7.8 million, including almost 2 million Arab Israelis, growing by immigration and high birth rates.

  10. According to Fig. 3.26, what happened to the Gaza Strip and the Sinai, and what are the "Occupied Territories"?

    Israel returned Gaza to Palestine in 2005; the Sinai went back to Egypt in 1982. The Occupied Territories include the West Bank and Golan Heights.

North Africa and Southwest Asia pp. 87–88

  1. Which country produces the world's largest refugee flow, and how big a share?

    Afghanistan, after more than 40 years of conflict: about a quarter of all refugees worldwide.

  2. What happened to Iraq's Kurds after the 1991 Gulf War?

    Threatened by Baghdad's forces, up to 1.85 million fled toward and into Turkey and Iran. A U.S.-allied secure zone in northern Iraq only partly brought them back.

  3. How many refugees and IDPs did the 2003 Iraq War produce?

    Over 2 million refugees (mostly in Syria and Jordan) and 2.8 million IDPs.

  4. What happened to Syrian Kurds after 2011, and what Iraqi law helped them?

    About 200,000 became refugees in Iraq, where a 1971 law gives refugees the right to stay and protection. (Iraq also hosts Iranian refugees, including the MEK, exiled in 1980.)

  5. What did the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (end of 1979) do to migration?

    Created a double migration stream: west into Iran (2.5 million in camps at the peak) and east into Pakistan (3.7 million in tent camps).

  6. After the Soviets withdrew (1989), what did most Afghan refugees do?

    Most of the more than 6 million in Iran and Pakistan, about one-quarter of Afghanistan's people, stayed where they were.

  7. Who are the Taliban, and how did their 1996 takeover affect migration?

    An Islamic fundamentalist movement from northwest Pakistan. Several hundred thousand refugees returned, but their harsh rule caused a counter-migration into Iran, where Afghan refugees reached 2.5 million.

  8. What happened to Afghan refugees around September 11, 2001?

    Afghanistan had become a base for the attackers. Before the U.S. response, tens of thousands crossed into Pakistan and Iran despite efforts to close the borders.

Sub-Saharan Africa pp. 88–89

  1. How has West Africa's refugee situation changed since 1997?

    Civil wars in Liberia and Sierra Leone sent 1.5 million refugees to Guinea and the Ivory Coast in 1997; by 2013, under 270,000, thanks to stability and repatriation.

  2. Why do Sudan's borders matter to its conflicts?

    They exist because of European colonialism and enclose a largely Arab, Muslim north and a south of Christianity and traditional religions.

  3. What was Sudan's second civil war (1983–2005), and what did it cost?

    North (Arab, Muslim) against south (Black African, Christian or animist); genocide and the Janjaweed's scorched earth. Over 2.2 million died; over 5 million displaced, 1.6 million to Uganda.

  4. What pushed Sudan's north to compromise, and when was a peace deal reached?

    Oil exports from the south began in 1999; world attention pushed the north to compromise. Temporary peace deal: 2002.

  5. What happened in Darfur, and why does the book say it shows conflict isn't just religious?

    The Arab Muslim government and the Janjaweed waged genocide against non-Arab Muslims: over 400,000 killed, 2.5 million displaced. It was Muslim against Muslim, so it's ethnic and political too.

  6. What share of northern Sudanese speak Arabic as their native language, and how many ethnic groups do Darfur's non-Arab Muslims belong to?

    Only two-thirds; the other third are Muslim but not ethnically Arab, in at least 30 ethnic groups.

  7. How does the 1948 Convention define genocide, and who called Darfur a genocide?

    Acts intended to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnic, racial or religious group. U.S. Secretary of State Colin Powell, in 2004.

  8. What happened after South Sudan voted to secede in 2011?

    The new border generated new refugee flows; a six-month border war in 2012; renewed violence displaced 1.3 million of 12 million people, with over 300,000 fleeing abroad.

  9. According to Fig. 3.29's caption, how many refugees live in camps, and who runs them?

    6.6 million: 4.6 million in camps run by the UNHCR and other NGOs; 2 million in camps they built themselves with little support. (Photo: Bredjing, Chad, Darfur refugees.)

  10. What has happened in the DR Congo since 1998?

    Just under 6 million died in violence spurred partly by refugee flows from Rwanda's 1994 war. In 2009, the Lord's Resistance Army generated over 1 million refugees.

South and Southeast Asia p. 89

  1. Where does South Asia rank for refugees, and why?

    Third among world regions, mainly because Pakistan hosts so many Afghan refugees (over 3 million in the 1980s; today more than 2 million Afghans live abroad, mostly in Pakistan and Iran).

  2. What caused Sri Lanka's civil war, and how many IDPs remain?

    The minority Tamils demanded an independent state on the Sinhalese-controlled island (war ended 2009). About 200,000 IDPs; about 90,000 still uprooted.

  3. Why is Bangladesh called "ground zero" for climate refugees?

    156.6 million people in a river delta one-fifth the size of France, mostly no more than 6.1 m (20 ft) above sea level, with 230 major rivers: exposed to flooding and typhoons. 17% may be underwater by 2050.

  4. How have people made Bangladesh more vulnerable, and how has India responded to its migrants?

    Groundwater pumping makes cities sink; mangrove deforestation removed storm-surge barriers. India is building a border wall to keep environmental refugees out.

  5. How many people fled Vietnam after its war ended, and when?

    1 to 2 million, after the war ended in 1975.

  6. What did Pol Pot and the Khmer Rouge do in Cambodia?

    Ruled 1975–1979 and committed genocide: 1.7 million killed (about a quarter of Cambodians), mainly in cities, targeting the educated and Western-influenced. Survivors fled to camps in Thailand, then the West.

  7. Who lives in Southeast Asia's largest camps today?

    IDPs in Myanmar: victims of the 2004 tsunami, the 2008 cyclone and generals suppressing minorities.

Europe and the Americas pp. 89–90

  1. What was Europe's largest refugee crisis since World War II (as of the book), and how many refugees did UNHCR report in 1995?

    The breakup of Yugoslavia in the 1990s: 6,056,600 refugees, which some thought was inflated. Over 100,000 IDPs remain after the Dayton Accords.

  2. How many IDPs does Colombia have, why, and when was it passed by Syria?

    About 5.7 million, from political violence tied to narcotics. It had the most IDPs in the world until 2013, when Syria had 2 million more.

  3. What did the 2010 earthquakes in Chile and Haiti do?

    Chile: hundreds killed, 2 million displaced. Haiti (six weeks earlier): 200,000 killed, 1.5 million displaced.

  4. What happened when Hurricanes Matthew and Irma hit Haiti?

    Matthew (2016) killed 546 and affected 2.1 million; Irma followed in 2017. 140,000 households still lacked earthquake-resistant shelter.

  5. What does the book call refugee and IDP populations?

    A barometer of the world's political strife.

The politics of migration p. 90

  1. How do political parties use migration, and what shapes how people perceive it?

    Parties take anti-immigration stances to get their base to vote. Perceptions depend on where you live (border areas differ) and how often you interact with migrants.

  2. What does the Great Wall of China show about migration politics?

    It's not new: in the 1300s, China built it for defense, but also to keep Chinese in and Mongols out.

  3. What was Australia's Immigration Restriction Act?

    A 1901 law ending all nonwhite immigration, targeting Japanese, Chinese and South Asian migrants and barring South Pacific Islanders from the sugar plantations: the White Australia Policy, until the 1970s.

Waves of immigration in the United States pp. 90–91

  1. What were the two waves of U.S. immigration in Fig. 3.30?

    First wave, 1820–1930: mostly from Europe. Second wave, 1930–present: mostly from Latin America and Asia.

  2. Where did U.S. immigrants come from between 1820 and 1900?

    Almost all from Europe.

  3. What happened to European migration after the Great Depression?

    It slowed, and Europe became a region that received more migrants than it sent (guest workers).

  4. When did the source of U.S. immigrants change, and why?

    In the 1960s, when new legislation passed. Since then, mainly from Latin America and Asia.

  5. What happened to U.S. immigration after 2008?

    It fell after the financial crisis.

Legal restrictions pp. 90–92

  1. Are most obstacles to migrants physical or legal?

    Legal.

  2. What were the first restrictive U.S. immigration laws?

    The Oriental Exclusion Acts (1882–1907), to stop Chinese immigration to California.

  3. Where did U.S. immigrants come from in the 1800s?

    Mostly northern (Scandinavia) and western Europe (Ireland, Great Britain, Germany, France); later in the century, more from southern and eastern Europe (Italy, Spain, Portugal, Russia, Poland).

  4. What was the political mood in the U.S. after World War I, and why did Congress restrict immigration?

    Isolationism (staying out of foreign entanglements). Congress feared growing migration from southern and eastern Europe; many whites saw southern Europeans as darker-skinned and inferior.

  5. What did the 1921 quota law do, and whom did it favor?

    Set quotas at 3% of each European nationality living in the U.S. in 1910, favoring northern and western Europe.

  6. What did the Immigration Act of 1924 change?

    Lowered the quota to 2%, made 1890 the base year, and cut the yearly total to 150,000, discouraging southern and eastern Europeans further.

  7. What did the 1929 National Origins Law do?

    Limited immigration to 150,000 a year and tied quotas to the national origins of the 1920 U.S. population, effectively blocking immigration from Asia.

  8. What happened to U.S. immigration in the 1930s, and why?

    It slowed to a trickle, because of the restrictive laws and the Great Depression.

  9. What does Fig. 3.31 show about the 1925–1927 quotas?

    The most migrants were allowed from western (Ireland, UK, Germany) and northern (Norway, Sweden, Denmark) Europe; a bias against southern (Portugal, Spain, Greece) and eastern Europeans.

  10. What was the Bracero Program?

    A 1942 program inviting Mexicans to work in U.S. agriculture during World War II. Until it ended in 1965, they went mainly to the Southwest and Florida.

  11. How did Mexican migration change after 1965?

    Under Bracero it was often cyclical (work the planting and harvest, then go home). After 1965, Mexican migrants crossed and stayed.

  12. When did Congress give China and Japan equal status with European countries?

    China in 1943; Japan in 1952.

  13. What did the 1952 Immigration and Nationality Act do, and why did far more people enter?

    Combined earlier laws, set quotas for all countries, capped the total at 160,000. Far more entered as refugees, filling quotas years ahead: maybe over 7 million, 1945–1970.

  14. What did Congress do in 1965?

    Called the 1952 act a failure and abolished the quota system: 170,000 a year from outside the Western Hemisphere, 120,000 from the Americas. It also ended the Bracero Program.

  15. What did the Immigration Reform and Control Act (IRCA) do, and when?

    In 1986, gave permanent residence and a path to citizenship to 2.6 million unauthorized migrants.

  16. Describe migration from Mexico and Central America to the U.S. since the 1970s.

    Accelerated in the 1970s and 1980s, peaked around 2000 at close to 1 million a year, then fell sharply after 2008.

  17. What share of the U.S. population are Mexican migrants, and what share of immigrants were Mexican before and after 2008?

    Close to 4% of the U.S. population. Almost 30% of immigrants before 2008; closer to 25% since.

Post–September 11 pp. 92–93

  1. How did U.S. immigration policy and the Border Patrol's focus change after 9/11?

    Policy now centers on security. Before, the Border Patrol focused mainly on drug trafficking and human smuggling.

  2. What was Operation Liberty Shield?

    A post-9/11 policy that automatically detained asylum seekers from 33 countries where al-Qaeda or other terrorist groups operate.

  3. Who criticized Operation Liberty Shield, and when did it end?

    Human Rights Watch (March 25, 2003), for suspicion based on nationality alone. The Department of Homeland Security quietly ended it on April 17, 2003.

  4. How do tighter U.S. border controls affect border regions?

    They affect not just migrants but business and commerce in border regions.

  5. How did Europe's response to terrorism (Madrid 2004, London 2005) differ from the U.S. approach?

    The U.S. took a "hard" approach, expanding controls. Europe limited unauthorized migration into its shared free-movement space while promoting policies to help migrants buy in to the host society.

  6. What is an integration agreement? Give the reading's example.

    A requirement some migrants must meet before permanent status and social welfare. Austria: 300 hours of language training and a civics course, hard on newly arrived migrants.

  7. What do opponents argue about U.S. and European security-based migration policies?

    They may do more harm than good by intensifying misunderstanding and hatred.
