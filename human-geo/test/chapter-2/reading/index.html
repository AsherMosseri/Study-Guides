Apply it

For a question about food or pressure on the land, use physiologic density; for how crowded a country is overall, arithmetic works. And a physiologic density that keeps rising even as farmland expands, as Egypt's does, is Malthus's worry at the scale of one country.

Population distribution pp. 6–7

People aren't spread evenly across the world, or even within a single region or country. One-third of all humans live in China and India, yet both countries have huge areas with very few people: the Himalayas in India and a vast desert interior in China.

Besides density, geographers study : a description of the pattern people make across space. That includes where they're (lots of people close together) and where they're (few people, spread out).

Cities usually started in farming areas, and people lived nearest the most productive land. Recently, better farming technology and easier shipping of food have loosened that link. Maps now show people crowding into cities while spreading out more thinly in the countryside.

To map distribution, geographers use , where each dot stands for a set number of people. Up close (local scale), one dot might show a single farm in an empty rural area. At the global scale, the picture gets much more generalized (Fig. 2.4).

Reliability of population data pp. 11–12

The U.S. runs a every 10 years, and before each one the government advertises to get every person counted. An undercount means less money for city governments.

Who gets missed? Research shows these groups are less likely to fill out a census form:

  • migrants
  • racial minorities
  • families who double up in rentals or have no home
  • low-income families

Advocates for these groups push people to return their forms. Their worry: groups that are already disadvantaged lose out even more when an undercount cuts funding for services.

Two recent controversies:

  • Advocacy groups have pushed the Census Bureau to sample the population and calculate the totals statistically, arguing it would be more accurate. The Bureau has stuck with its traditional method: trying to count every single person within U.S. borders.
  • Supporters say the census should distinguish citizens from unauthorized (undocumented) immigrants. Opponents say the question would scare off anyone with legal worries about citizenship, producing an inaccurate count.

These debates, plus the cost of counting everyone, make accurate counts hard. Several organizations still collect population data by country:

  • the United Nations, which gathers and publishes official statistics from national governments
  • the World Bank
  • the Population Reference Bureau

2.2 Identify and Explain Influences on Population Growth over Time pp. 12–31

Worry about "too many people" is not new. In the late 1960s, set off alarms worldwide by arguing that population was growing faster than food production. But that fear goes back much further, to an essay a British economist published in 1798.

Malthus pp. 12–13

published An Essay on the Principles of Population in 1798. His warning: population was outrunning the food supply needed to keep people alive. His reasoning rested on two kinds of growth:

  • Food grows linearly: a little more farmland and a few more crops are added each year.
  • Population grows exponentially: (See the world population chart under Doubling time for the real numbers.)

He kept revising the essay from 1803 to 1826 and fought back hard against critics. His predictions assumed that

Why Malthus was wrong (so far):

  • He didn't foresee globalization: farm products being traded all over the world.
  • connected the Americas, Europe, Africa, Asia and the Pacific. Along the way, farming methods spread, and crops and livestock crossed oceans to new places whose climates and soils suited them.
  • Countries with little arable land could import all kinds of crops. , getting around its limits on growing food.
  • More land has been cultivated, and better seeds, pesticides, fertilizers, irrigation and constant innovation have raised yields per acre enormously. In the 2000s, bioengineering keeps adding new hybrids, genetically modified organisms (GMOs) and new fertilizers.

But the debate isn't over. , which raises real doubts about whether agriculture can keep expanding forever. have revived Malthus's ideas, but they focus far more on population growth than on food:

  • Many demographers expect world population to level off later this century.
  • Neo-Malthusians argue overpopulation is still a real problem that will cause human suffering.
  • They doubt Earth can support many more people, and expect growth to be stopped when we hit the limits of resources: not just food, but also energy and water.

Doubling time pp. 21–22

is how long a population takes to double at its current growth rate. Every growth rate has one.

So a 10% growth rate means a doubling time of roughly 7 years.

How fast world population doubled:

  • 2,000 years ago: about 250 million people.
  • 1650: 500 million. That first doubling took more than 16 centuries.
  • 1820 (Malthus was still writing): 1 billion, only 170 years later (Fig. 2.13).
  • 1930: 2 billion, just over a century later. The doubling time had dropped to about 100 years and was still falling fast.
  • 1975: 4 billion, only 45 years later.
  • Mid-1980s: doubling time was only 39 years.
  • Since then: policies to slow growth, including China's one-child policy, stretched it back out to 54 years.

In richer countries, more women stay in school longer, build careers and marry later, so they have children later. That makes the population older, which raises the : the number of people over 65 compared with the working-age population (ages 15–64).

  • Europe: 29.9 old-age dependents per 100 working-age people, expected to hit 47 by 2050.
  • Sub-Saharan Africa: only 5.7.

  • Older people retire and eventually have health problems, so they need pensions and medical care.
  • Younger workers pay the taxes that fund those services.
  • As the share of older people grows, the share of younger people shrinks.
  • So aging countries have fewer young taxpayers supporting more and more retirees.

Immigrants tend to be young workers who pay taxes on their wages, homes and purchases.

  • Japan's population has stopped growing and is projected to keep shrinking.
  • It fell from a peak of 128.06 million in 2008 to 126.8 million in 2017.
  • Japan expects about 100 million by 2050, a loss of 20%.
  • Japan was closed to outsiders for centuries, and its government still discourages immigration. Over 98% of its people are ethnically Japanese, per government statistics.

  • In some lower-income countries, government and nongovernmental (NGO) programs together encourage women to have fewer children.
  • Some women also choose fewer children because of economic and social uncertainty.

Population pyramids pp. 25–28

Geographers care about more than distribution and growth. They also study : the makeup of a population by age, sex and other traits like marital status and education. Age and sex are the most important, and they're shown with , graphs of a population's age and sex structure (Fig. 2.15).

  1. Along the bottom (horizontal) axis: males on the left, females on the right.
  2. Up the side (vertical) axis: age groups, usually 5-year steps.
  3. The youngest group (starting at age 0) is at the bottom; the oldest is at the top.
  4. Every pyramid starts at 0, but they don't all end at the same age. Countries with longer life expectancies have more people in the top bracket, so always check the top bracket.

Lower-income countries (high birth rates and high death rates):

  • The youngest groups are the biggest share of the population.
  • In most lower-income countries, the three groups up to age 14 make up more than 40% of everyone.
  • From ages 15–19 upward, each group is smaller than the one below it.
  • The three oldest groups are under 10% of the total.
  • Close variations on this shape: Pakistan, Yemen, Guatemala, Cameroon and Laos.

Stage 1: Low growth p. 29

  • Stage 1 is the starting phase, where every place spent most of human history.
  • It's unpredictable: high birth rates and equally high death rates.
  • Epidemics and plagues keep death rates high, sometimes higher than birth rates.

Stage 2: High growth pp. 29–30

In Europe:

  • Stage 2 began after the Industrial Revolution (around 1750).
  • Better access to food, sanitation and health care sharply lowered death rates.
  • Farming improvements that came before the Industrial Revolution made food supplies more stable.
  • Sanitation made towns and cities safer from epidemics, soap came into wider use, and modern medicine started to catch on.

  • Before 1750, European death rates may have averaged about 35 per 1,000, with birth rates under 40.
  • By 1850, Britain's death rate was down to about 16 per 1,000.
  • Death rates fell fast while birth rates stayed high, and Britain's population exploded.

Today:

  • Stage 2 countries are much lower-income countries, with fairly high birth rates and natural increase and slowly falling death rates.

Stage 3: Moderate growth p. 30

  • Birth rates start dropping but stay above death rates, so the population still grows, just more slowly.
  • In Great Britain, this was roughly 1870 through the two world wars of the 1900s.
  • New opportunities, especially for women, often didn't fit with having large families.
  • Women put off marriage and having children.
  • Medical advances cut infant and child deaths, so families no longer felt they needed many children to make sure some survived.
  • Young adults married later, which naturally lowered TFR and crude birth rates.
  • Stage 3 starts when a country's birth rate begins to fall.
  • Today: middle-income countries like Brazil.
  • Studies in Mali found girls who attend school end up with just over half as many children as women with no schooling. Expanding education for girls and women there will likely have a big effect on future growth.

Stage 4: Low growth pp. 30–31

  • In Great Britain after 1950, both rates fell to low levels, so growth slowed or stopped.
  • Better modern contraceptives and wider access to legal abortion after the 1950s helped keep birth rates very low in Britain and other higher-income countries.
  • Today: higher-income countries like the United States and United Kingdom.
  • Birth rates there are low because contraceptives spread, abortion became available, and many women decided to have fewer children, none at all, or children later in life.

Where the model falls short

The DTM was built from one region's history: western Europe after the Industrial Revolution. The reading shows other countries taking different routes:

  • Deaths can fall without industrializing first. Today's stage 2 countries, like Afghanistan and the Palestinian territories, lowered death rates partly because western medicine and childhood vaccines spread to them (diffusion, from Chapter 1). In Britain, deaths fell with its own industrialization.
  • Births fall by different routes. Britain's fell as people married later. Brazil's fell because of modern contraceptives, not later marriage. Iran's TFR fell from 6.8 to 1.7 once the government allowed family planning.
  • Low growth doesn't always mean development. Russia reached negative growth through a crisis after 1991, not through wealth. In parts of sub-Saharan Africa, HIV/AIDS held growth down by raising deaths.
  • It leaves out migration. The model tracks only births and deaths. An aging country can add young workers through immigration.
  • It can't tell you when. The World Bank's late-1980s forecasts of when countries would stop growing turned out to be unrealistic.
Apply it

Afghanistan is in stage 2, but its death rate fell partly because vaccines arrived from abroad, not because it industrialized the way Britain did after 1750. Russia is in stage 5 alongside Japan, but it got there through a crisis, not through wealth and long lives. The stages describe birth and death rates well, but they don't always explain why a country is where it is.

Section 2.2 in a nutshell pp. 54–55

  • Malthus warned in the late 1700s that Britain would face mass famine from rapid growth. It didn't happen, but fast world growth since then has won his ideas new followers. His argument works better at the global scale than for single places, since trade between places weakens the link between one place's population and its own food. Even globally, rising farm output complicates his logic.
  • TFR (average children per woman) shows how fast populations grow. Below 2.1 means not growing. More and more countries, especially rich ones, aren't growing, which makes it harder for working-age people to support children and the elderly.
  • Population pyramids show age and sex structure. They got the name because early on, most countries had the most children, then fewer young adults, older adults and elderly, forming a pyramid. Wealthier countries' pyramids today aren't pyramid-shaped, because their biggest groups are middle-aged.
  • The demographic transition model explains the huge growth of recent centuries: death rates fall while birth rates stay high, and growth slows once birth rates fall too. Birth rates fell first in more urban, wealthy countries. They're falling nearly everywhere now, but remain fairly high in poorer, less urban places.
  • Women's education and opportunities track closely with lower natural increase, and will be central to population trends in the coming decades.

  • : the chance that a baby dies before its first birthday.
  • : the chance that a child dies before age 5.
  • Both are given as deaths per 1,000 live births.
  • : how many babies die in their first month of life for every 1,000 live births.

  • In the United States, which the report says has a high newborn death rate, and in other higher-income countries, newborn deaths mostly come from babies born too early (premature) or too small (low birthweight).
  • In lower-income countries, half of all newborn deaths (deaths before 1 month old) are caused by diarrhea and infections.

  • A major factor is the mother's physical health, which includes whether she can get prenatal care (care during pregnancy) and whether she has sanitation and hygiene.
  • In societies where most women have many babies, women also tend to be poorly fed and poorly educated.
  • Diarrhea and malnutrition are the top killers of infants and children around the world.
  • A mother may then eat too little for her baby to reach a healthy birth weight, or, once the baby is born, to make nourishing breast milk.

  • About 30% of the world's people can't easily get clean drinking water.

Infectious disease vs. chronic disease p. 40

Look back over this chapter's world maps: population growth rates, total fertility rate (TFR), infant mortality and life expectancy. North America, Europe, Japan, and Australia and New Zealand sit in one camp. Latin America, Africa and much of Asia sit in the other.

Income doesn't just change how many people are born and how long they live. It also changes which diseases people tend to catch. That idea has a name:

  • : as a country moves from fast population growth to stable growth, two things about disease change. What people die of changes, and how old they are when disease strikes changes.
  • Countries with high birth rates and fast growth have more infectious diseases, and those diseases hit younger people.
  • Countries with stable growth (low birth rates and low death rates) have more chronic diseases, and those hit older people.

Diseases come in three basic types:

  • happen when parasites get into the body and multiply there. Scientists estimate that 65% of all diseases are infectious.
  • come with old age. The reading's examples: heart disease, prostate cancer and diabetes.
  • trace back to a person's genes. Examples: sickle-cell anemia, hemophilia and cystic fibrosis. Medical geographers care about them because they tend to show up in particular places and particular populations.

Malaria and other vectored diseases pp. 41–43

  1. A mosquito bites a person or animal that already has the disease. That infected person or animal is called a host.
  2. The mosquito takes in blood that carries the malaria parasites.
  3. The parasites breed inside the mosquito and end up in its saliva.
  4. When the mosquito bites its next victim, it injects some parasites into that person's blood.
  5. The parasites multiply inside the new victim, who now has malaria and becomes a host too.
  • Survivors build up some immunity. But many infected people are left weak and low on energy, and other diseases can take hold more easily.
  • Tropical Africa suffers most. It is also common in the tropical Americas and parts of India and Southeast Asia (Fig. 2.24).

2.4 Identify Why and How Governments Influence Population Growth pp. 51–54

Governments don't just count their people. Many try to change them. aim at one of two targets:

  • how fast the population grows, or
  • its ethnic composition: which ethnic groups make up the population.

  • To lower birth rates: paying part of the cost of abortions (subsidized abortion) or forcing people to be sterilized.
  • To raise birth rates: tax breaks for families with children (tax incentives) and advertising (marketing campaigns).

The book sorts government population policies into three categories:

  • policies encourage people to have more children.
  • policies favor one racial or cultural group while discouraging other groups from having children.
  • policies try to lower the rate of natural increase.

The rule: pyramid names (expansive, stationary, constrictive) describe a shape. Policy names (expansive, eugenic, restrictive; AP: pronatalist, antinatalist) describe a government's goal. Memory hook: an expansive policy is what a country uses when its pyramid is no longer expansive; it is trying to widen the base again. A restrictive policy tries to narrow a base that is too wide. Also don't confuse a stationary pyramid with the book's "stationary population level" (p. 31), which means a population that has stopped growing.

Apply it

If a question shows a straight-sided, chimney-shaped pyramid like Sweden's (the book names Sweden as a chimney country) and asks which policy fits, the answer is an expansive (pronatalist) policy, like Sweden's cash payments, tax incentives and job leave for parents. If it shows a wide-based pyramid like Niger's (Fig. 2.15), the matching policy is restrictive (antinatalist). Don't let the shared word "expansive" trick you into matching an expansive pyramid with an expansive policy.

Apply it

Take Niger. The book never assigns it a stage, but a total fertility rate of 7.2 children per woman (2016) points to stage 2, so you can predict the rest: an expansive pyramid with nearly half the population under 15 (measured from Fig. 2.15), a high infant mortality rate, deaths mostly from infectious diseases that hit children, a heavy child dependency burden, and, if the government acts, a restrictive policy like Indonesia's. Japan, which the book places in stage 5, is the mirror image: a very low fertility rate, a constrictive pyramid, the lowest infant mortality rate among large countries (1.9 deaths per 1,000 live births), chronic diseases of old age, too few young taxpayers, and campaigns to raise births.

Three links from the book tie section 2.3 to 2.2:

  • Fewer infant deaths lead to fewer births. In stage 3, medical progress cut infant and child deaths, so families no longer felt they needed many children (p. 30). The map of infant mortality (Fig. 2.21) matches the map of birth rates (Fig. 2.7).
  • Fewer deaths at first mean faster growth. Stage 2's population explosion came from falling death rates (better food, sanitation, soap, medicine, vaccines) while births stayed high (pp. 29–30). World life expectancy rose from 30 years (1900) to 72 years (2016), which is part of why world population grew (p. 16).
  • The causes of death change as growth slows. Fast-growing countries have more infectious diseases that strike the young; stable countries have more chronic diseases that strike the old (the epidemiological transition, p. 40).
