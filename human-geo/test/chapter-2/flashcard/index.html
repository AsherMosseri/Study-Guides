Opening story: Shanghai pp. 1–3

  1. How tall is the Shanghai Tower, and what did the author see from the top?

    It's 128 stories, over 2,000 ft (600 m) tall. Even on a sunny day the city below was hard to see through the smog.

  2. How many people live in China and in Shanghai, and how many Chinese cities top 1 million?

    China has 1.4 billion people. Shanghai, the biggest city, has about 22 million. More than 100 Chinese cities have over 1 million people.

  3. What two things happening at once drove China's rapid urbanization, and what did it cost?

    China's economy and its population grew fast at the same time. That rapid urbanization hurt both the environment and people's health.

  4. What is PM-2.5, where does China's come from, and why is it so harmful?

    PM-2.5 is a fine-particle air pollutant released by burning coal, China's main fuel. Bigger particles mostly irritate the eyes and throat, but PM-2.5 is small enough to damage the lungs and pass into the bloodstream.

  5. How much shorter is life expectancy in northern China than in the south, and why?

    Life expectancy in northern China is about 5.5 years shorter than in the south, because the air pollution is worse in the north.

  6. Name China's three responses to its pollution.

    It limited how much coal can be burned, banned imports of plastics brought in to be burned, and limited the number of cars in major cities.

  7. What is the Shanghai story setting up for the chapter?

    The chapter studies population trends at different scales and ties population growth to health, because the two are closely linked everywhere.

What population geographers study pp. 3–4

  1. What is demography, and how do population geographers use it?

    Demography is the study of overall population trends. Population geographers team up with demographers to explain how and why those trends differ from place to place.

  2. Why does scale matter when you study population? Give the reading's example.

    A pattern you can see at a small scale can disappear at a larger one. People moving from downtown to the suburbs in one city would never show up on a world map.

  3. What did the Yangon field note show about crowding and natural hazards?

    When many people live close together in poor places with weak infrastructure, natural hazards hit them much harder.

  4. What happened near Yangon in 2008?

    A tropical cyclone struck the Irrawaddy delta south of Yangon, killing about 100,000 people and leaving millions homeless.

  5. A single number for a whole country (density, growth or life expectancy) looks moderate. What should you ask, and what are two examples from the reading?

    Ask what the national average hides at a smaller scale, inside the country. Egypt's arithmetic density of 252 people per sq mi hides that 98% of people live on 3% of the land. China's national life expectancy hides a 5.5-year gap between the polluted north and the south.

Population density pp. 4–5

  1. What is population density, and what does it assume?

    Population density is the number of people compared with the amount of land. It assumes everyone is spread out evenly.

  2. What is arithmetic population density? Give the U.S. numbers.

    Arithmetic population density is the number of people divided by all the land. The U.S. has 326 million people on 5,692,815 sq mi (9,161,966 sq km), so its arithmetic density is just over 86 people per sq mi (33 per sq km).

  3. What are the arithmetic densities of Bangladesh, the Netherlands and Japan?

    Bangladesh has 2,962 people per sq mi, the Netherlands 1,068 and Japan 869.

  4. Why can arithmetic density mislead? Give two examples.

    No country's people are really spread evenly. The U.S. average hides how empty Alaska and much of the West are, and Egypt's hides that nearly everyone lives along the Nile.

  5. What are Egypt's population, arithmetic density and land area, and where do most Egyptians actually live?

    Egypt has 97.6 million people. Its arithmetic density is 252 people per sq mi (97 per sq km), over a land area of 384,345 sq mi (995,450 sq km) that is mostly desert. Nearly 98% of Egyptians live on about 3% of the land: the Nile valley and delta.

  6. What does the Luxor field note show about what shapes where people live?

    Culture shapes where people live and what they do, but the physical environment shapes it too. Near Luxor, green irrigated fields line the Nile, and a short walk away it's desert.

Physiologic density p. 6

  1. What is physiologic population density? What is Egypt's?

    Physiologic population density is the number of people per unit of arable (farmable) land. Egypt's is 6,995 people per sq mi (2,701 per sq km), and it keeps rising even as Egypt tries to expand its irrigated farmland.

  2. Why is Switzerland's physiologic density 10 times its arithmetic density?

    It's mountainous: only 1 acre in 10 is arable.

  3. What are Ukraine's population, arithmetic density and physiologic density?

    Ukraine has 44.8 million people. Its arithmetic density is 192 people per sq mi (74 per sq km), and its physiologic density is 281 people per sq mi of arable land (109 per sq km). The physiologic density is 1.46 times the arithmetic because 1 of every 1.46 acres is arable.

  4. What does the gap between a country's arithmetic and physiologic densities tell you?

    The gap shows what share of the country's land is farmable. A big gap means little farmland.

  5. Which regions have high and moderate physiologic densities, and where does India stand in South Asia?

    Middle America and the Caribbean have high physiologic densities, and South America's are moderate. India has the lowest physiologic density in South Asia despite its huge population.

  6. China and India both have over 1 billion people. Which has more farmland per person?

    India has much more farmland per person than China, as their physiologic densities show.

  7. A country's physiologic density is many times its arithmetic density. What does that tell you, and how can the country feed its people?

    Very little of its land can be farmed, so each acre carries a heavy load. Egypt's arithmetic density is 252 people per sq mi, but its physiologic density is 6,995. The country can add farmland, grow more on each acre, or import food, as Norway (2% arable) does.

  8. What is agricultural density, and how do rural Bangladesh and Iowa show the difference?

    Agricultural density is the number of farmers per unit of farmland (an AP term the book doesn't use). Rural Bangladesh, where almost everyone is a small farmer, has a very high agricultural density; Iowa, where farm technology lets a few people work the land, has a very low one.

Population distribution pp. 6–7

  1. What share of humanity lives in China and India, and where are those countries nearly empty?

    One-third of the world's people live in China and India. Parts of both are nearly empty: the Himalayas in India and the desert interior of China.

  2. What is population distribution? What do clustered and dispersed mean?

    Population distribution is the pattern people make across space. A clustered distribution has many people close together. A dispersed one has few people, spread out.

  3. How has the link between farmland and where people live changed?

    For most of history people lived where they could grow food, and cities started in farming areas. Better farm technology and easier shipping of food loosened that link: people now crowd into cities and thin out in the countryside.

  4. What is a dot map, and what does one dot stand for on Fig. 2.4?

    Each dot stands for a set number of people; on Fig. 2.4, 1 dot = 100,000 people. At a local scale a dot can mark a single farm; at the global scale the picture is much more generalized.

  5. What is a dasymetric map (Fig. 2.5)?

    A dasymetric map uses landscape, land cover and topography to estimate where people actually cluster and where they're sparse, instead of spreading them evenly.

  6. Rank the world's four biggest population clusters, from largest to smallest.

    East Asia is the largest, then South Asia, then Europe, then North America.

  7. On a population map, what does a thin ribbon of people through empty land tell you? What about a cluster that ends sharply?

    A ribbon follows a river's water and farmland (the Nile; the Huang He and Yangtze), so the country's physiologic density is far above its arithmetic density. A sharp edge means a physical barrier, like South Asia's Himalayas and Indian Ocean.

  8. What does a dot map show well, what does a dasymetric map add, and what does a map shaded country by country hide?

    A dot map shows where people are. A dasymetric map uses land cover to show how crowded places really are (density, not totals). A country-shaded map hides differences inside a country, like India's north and south.

  9. What is a choropleth map, and which maps in the reading are choropleths?

    A choropleth map shades each area, here each country, by a rate or value (an AP term the book doesn't use). The reading's world maps of natural increase, birth rates and death rates (Figs. 2.6–2.8) are choropleths. They're good for comparing countries but hide differences inside each one, like India's north and south.

The four big clusters pp. 8–11

  1. Which countries make up the East Asia population cluster, what share of the world's people live there, and where do they concentrate?

    The East Asia cluster is mostly China, plus Korea and Japan. It holds almost ¼ of the world's people, with 1.4 billion in China alone. Besides the big cities, dense "ribbons" of people follow the Huang He and Yangtze valleys, where farmers grow wheat and rice for themselves and for cities like Shanghai and Beijing.

  2. How many people live in the South Asia population cluster, which countries make it up, and which river basins are crowded?

    The South Asia cluster has 1.5 billion people. India is its core, plus Pakistan, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka. People crowd into big cities, onto the coasts and into the Ganges, Indus and Brahmaputra basins.

  3. What physical barriers hem in South Asia's cluster?

    The Himalayas to the north, the mountains of Afghanistan and Pakistan to the west, and the Indian Ocean to the south and east.

  4. What are India's and China's population growth rates, and what was predicted for India by 2030?

    India's growth rate is 1.17%, higher than China's 0.56%. India was predicted to be the most populous country in the world by 2030, home to 1 of every 6 people on Earth.

  5. How does rural population density in Bangladesh compare with rural density in Iowa?

    Rural Bangladesh is far more crowded. Bangladesh fits over 156 million people, nearly all small farmers, into an area about the size of Iowa, with 3,000–5,000 people per sq mi in much of its countryside. Iowa had about 3.15 million people in 2017, and its rural density was 55 people per sq mi.

  6. How many people live in the Europe population cluster, and how is its pattern different from Asia's?

    Europe has over 725 million people, less than half as many as South Asia. In Asia, dense population follows coasts and rivers more closely; Europe is dense even in rugged, mountainous country. Europe was the hearth of the Industrial Revolution and the second urban revolution, so it industrialized and urbanized before Asia.

  7. Where did Europe's cities grow, and how urban are Germany, the UK and France?

    Europe's cities grew in the industrial zones of the 1800s and 1900s: northern France, western Germany, northern Italy and east into Russia. Germany is 77% urban, the UK 83% and France 80%. Europe's countryside is emptier than rural Asia's.

  8. How many people live in the three biggest population clusters (East Asia, South Asia and Europe) combined, and what comparison shows how big they are?

    About 4 billion of the world's 7.7 billion people live in East Asia, South Asia and Europe combined. No other cluster is even half the size of any of them; South America and Africa together barely outnumber India.

  9. What is a megalopolis, and where is North America's?

    A megalopolis is a giant continuous urban region where one city runs into the next. North America's runs from Boston to Washington, D.C. in the U.S. and from Quebec City through Montreal, Ottawa and Toronto to Windsor in Canada.

  10. How many people live in North America's megalopolis, what shares of Canadians and Americans live in its two corridors, and how does it compare in size with Europe's cluster?

    More than 70 million people live in the cities of North America's megalopolis, counting both corridors together. Half of all Canadians live in the Quebec City–Windsor corridor, and about 20% of Americans live in the Boston–Washington, D.C. corridor. Together the two corridors make a population cluster about ¼ the size of Europe's.

  11. Rank New York City, Mumbai and Dhaka by population density, from highest to lowest.

    Dhaka is the densest at 122,700 people per sq mi, then Mumbai at 68,400, then New York City at 28,717.

  12. Which of the big clusters is growing fastest and which is shrinking? How does that fit where their people live?

    South Asia grows fastest (India's growth rate is 1.17% vs. China's 0.56%) and is crowded even in the countryside. Europe, mostly urban, has negative natural increase, so without immigration it would shrink. The more urbanized a population, the lower its natural increase tends to be.

  13. Rural Bangladesh has 3,000–5,000 people per sq mi; rural Iowa has 55. What does the difference tell you about each place?

    Almost everyone in Bangladesh is a small farmer, so the countryside is packed. In Iowa, farm technology and shipping food let a few people work the land, so the countryside is thin. A crowded countryside signals a less urbanized country.

Reliability of population data pp. 11–12

  1. How often does the U.S. take a census, and why do states and cities want everyone counted?

    Every 10 years. Much federal funding is handed out by population, and each state's seats in Congress depend on the count, so an undercount costs money and representation.

  2. Which groups are least likely to be counted?

    Migrants, racial minorities, families who double up in rentals or have no home, and low-income families.

  3. What is the sampling debate?

    Advocacy groups want the Census Bureau to sample the population and calculate totals statistically, which they say is more accurate. The Bureau sticks to trying to count every person.

  4. What is the citizenship-question debate?

    Supporters want the census to separate citizens from unauthorized immigrants. Opponents say the question would scare people off and make the count inaccurate.

  5. Which three organizations collect population data by country, and what's the catch?

    The United Nations, the World Bank and the Population Reference Bureau. Their numbers don't always match: many figures are informed estimates, not counts.

Malthus and his critics pp. 12–13

  1. Who wrote The Population Bomb, when, and what did it argue?

    Paul Ehrlich wrote it in the late 1960s. He argued that population was growing faster than food production.

  2. What did Malthus argue in 1798, and what were his two kinds of growth?

    In An Essay on the Principles of Population, Malthus argued that population was outrunning the food supply. He said food grows linearly, but population grows exponentially.

  3. What did Malthus assume, and how long did he keep revising the essay?

    Malthus assumed that what a country eats depends on what it grows. He kept revising the essay from 1803 to 1826.

  4. Why was Malthus wrong (so far)?

    Countries aren't closed systems: he didn't foresee globalization and worldwide trade in food. Mercantilism, colonialism and capitalism connected the continents and spread farming methods, crops and livestock.

  5. Give the reading's two examples of food crossing borders.

    The potato came from South America; Irish farmers only started growing it in the 1700s. Norway has just 2% arable land but imports most of its food.

  6. What made food production explode?

    Food production exploded because more land was farmed; seeds, pesticides, fertilizers and irrigation got better; and in the 2000s bioengineering brought new hybrids, GMOs and new fertilizers.

  7. Who are the neo-Malthusians, and what do they argue?

    They revive Malthus but focus on population growth more than food. They say overpopulation will cause suffering and growth will hit the limits of food, energy and water. Farming's environmental costs back up their doubts.

  8. What is carrying capacity, and how does it connect to Malthus and the neo-Malthusians?

    Carrying capacity is the largest population an area's resources can support (an AP term the book doesn't use). Malthus worried food would set that limit; neo-Malthusians say limits of food, energy and water will stop growth. Critics answer that trade (Norway imports most of its food) and higher yields let places support more people.

Natural increase pp. 13–16

  1. What are the crude birth rate and crude death rate?

    The crude birth rate (CBR) is the number of live births per year for every 1,000 people. The crude death rate (CDR) is the number of deaths per year for every 1,000 people.

  2. How do you get the natural increase rate, and what does it leave out?

    Subtract the crude death rate from the crude birth rate (CBR − CDR), then divide by 10 to turn it into a percent. It leaves out migration: immigration and emigration.

  3. How do you measure a place's total population change, not just its natural increase?

    Start with natural increase (births minus deaths), then add people moving in (immigration) and subtract people moving out (emigration). Natural increase alone leaves migration out, so a place with more deaths than births can still grow if enough people move in.

  4. Which places have negative natural increase?

    Europe and Russia. Without immigration they'd be shrinking. The reading also has Japan shrinking, and negative growth in Thailand and Ukraine.

  5. What happened to growth in the Soviet Union?

    It grew fast in the mid-1900s, then growth dropped sharply after the breakup in 1991.

  6. How have India's and Africa's growth rates changed places?

    Thirty years before the book was written, India grew at nearly 3%, faster than most African countries. Now Africa grows 2.43% vs. India's 1.17%.

  7. How has HIV/AIDS affected parts of sub-Saharan Africa?

    It killed millions, orphaned children, cut life expectancy and held down growth.

  8. Which Muslim countries still grow fast, and which have dropped sharply?

    Sudan (2.55% a year), Yemen (2.52%), Afghanistan (2.65%) and the Palestinian territories (2.83%) still grow fast. Iran, Oman and Morocco have dropped sharply.

  9. What social pattern goes with high natural increase?

    High natural increase tends to go with customs that limit women's education and careers, with men running family life.

  10. How do population growth rates compare across Asia?

    India grows slightly below the world average but faster than China. China grows well below the world average, and Japan is shrinking. Southeast Asia grows faster but has far fewer people; Indonesia and Vietnam are slowing, and Thailand's growth is negative.

  11. How fast is South America's population growing, and how has Brazil's growth rate changed?

    South America as a whole grows a little over 1% a year. Brazil's growth rate fell from 2.9% in the mid-1960s to 0.8%. Argentina, Chile and Uruguay grow well below the world average.

  12. Where are population growth rates lowest?

    Growth rates are lowest in the global economic core: the U.S., Canada, Europe and Japan, plus Australia and Uruguay.

  13. Why did Russia's population shrink after 1991, and what is its situation now?

    Russia shrank because of worsening health, alcoholism and drug use, more male suicides and economic trouble. Its economy has improved since, but its birth rate is still low. Ukraine also has negative growth.

  14. What was world population in 1900, 2000 and 2019, and how did world life expectancy change from 1900 to 2016?

    World population grew from 1.6 billion in 1900 to 6.1 billion in 2000 and over 7.7 billion in 2019. World life expectancy rose from 30 years in 1900 to 72 years in 2016.

  15. When and at what number do demographers expect world population to level off?

    At 10 to 11 billion, sometime between 2050 and 2100.

  16. Growth can slow for two very different reasons. What are they? Give an example of each from the reading.

    Births fall (Brazil's contraceptives; Iran allowing family planning), which usually comes with development. Or deaths rise (HIV/AIDS in parts of sub-Saharan Africa; Russia after 1991, where worse health and more suicides raised deaths while births stayed low), which signals a crisis.

  17. How can rising life expectancy both speed up population growth and make a population older?

    Longer lives mean fewer deaths each year, so the population grows (world life expectancy rose from 30 years in 1900 to 72 in 2016). Once births fall too, those longer lives leave a bigger share of older people.

India pp. 17–20

  1. How is India divided, and which parts grow fastest?

    India is divided into 29 states and 7 union territories. Its northern states grow far faster than average; its southern and western states grow much more slowly.

  2. Why do India's south and west grow more slowly?

    Women there have higher literacy, more land ownership, better health care and more access to birth control.

  3. What world first did India achieve in 1952?

    In 1952 India became the first country in the world with a population planning program.

  4. What did India's government do in 1976 to slow population growth, and how did it end?

    India ran a mass drive to forcibly sterilize men with 3 or more children. Officials working under quotas also brought in childless men. The state of Maharashtra sterilized 3.7 million people before riots made the government drop the program. Nationwide the 1976 program sterilized 6 million men.

  5. How did India's policy change after the protests?

    Forced sterilization ended. The national government sets goals and each state sets its own policy (Maharashtra pays newlyweds to wait 2 years before a first child). The focus moved to birth control for women: injectables nationwide, small-family posters and clinics in remote villages.

  6. What is the contraceptive prevalence rate? Give India's rate and the reading's state examples.

    It is the percentage of women ages 15–49 who use at least one birth control method (or whose partner does). India's rate is 55.1%. Southern states are higher: Andhra Pradesh 69.8% and Maharashtra 63.5%. Northern states are lower: Bihar 26.0% and Meghalaya 21.1%.

  7. What's the lesson of the India case?

    A world map is only an introduction: the global scale hides what's happening inside each country.

Doubling time pp. 21–22

  1. What is doubling time? Use the money example.

    Doubling time is how long a population takes to double at its current growth rate. Money works the same way: $100 growing 10% a year becomes $200 in about 7 years, $400 in 14 years and $800 in 21.

  2. Give the world population milestones from 2,000 years ago to 1975.

    About 2,000 years ago the world had about 250 million people. It took more than 16 centuries to double to 500 million (1650). It reached 1 billion in 1820, 2 billion in 1930 and 4 billion in 1975, only 45 years later.

  3. What was the world's doubling time in the mid-1980s, and what is it now?

    The world's doubling time was only 39 years in the mid-1980s. Policies like China's one-child rule have since stretched it to 54 years.

  4. Why does doubling time matter less to demographers now?

    Populations are falling in many places, and the lengthening doubling time signals a slowdown this century.

  5. What explains the differences in growth, and how does urbanization fit in?

    No single factor: prosperity and social upheaval can both cut growth. Wealth brings urbanization, education, later marriage and family planning. The more urbanized a population, the lower its natural increase.

  6. How do you estimate a population's doubling time from its growth rate?

    Use the rule of 70: divide 70 by the yearly growth rate in percent. At 2% a year a population doubles in about 35 years; at 1%, about 70 years; at 0.5%, about 140 years. A population that isn't growing never doubles.

Total fertility and aging pp. 22–25

  1. What is the total fertility rate, and what is replacement level?

    The total fertility rate (TFR) is the average number of children born to women ages 15–49. Replacement level is a TFR of 2.1, enough to keep a population stable without immigration.

  2. How many countries have a total fertility rate below replacement level, and what share of the world's people live in them?

    More than 95 countries, home to 41% of the world's people, have fallen below replacement level.

  3. What does the UN predict for the world's total fertility rate by 2050, and what was the world TFR and its range in 2016?

    The UN predicts the world's TFR will fall below 2.2 by 2050. In 2016 the world TFR was 2.4, ranging from 1.2 children per woman in South Korea to 7.2 in Niger.

  4. What is the old-age dependency ratio? Compare Europe and sub-Saharan Africa.

    It compares people over 65 with working-age people (15–64). Europe has 29.9 people over 65 for every 100 of working age, heading to 47 by 2050; sub-Saharan Africa has 5.7. Africa's burden is children instead: its child dependency ratio is 74, vs. Europe's 23.

  5. Why is an aging population a problem, and what are the two fixes?

    Retirees need pensions and medical care, paid for by fewer and fewer young taxpayers. Fixes: raise the TFR, or allow immigration (immigrants tend to be young, taxpaying workers).

  6. How has Japan's population changed since its peak, what is it expected to be by 2050, and what is Japan's stance on immigration?

    Japan's population peaked at 128.06 million in 2008 and fell to 126.8 million by 2017. Japan expects about 100 million by 2050, a 20% loss. It still discourages immigration; over 98% of the population is Japanese, according to government statistics.

  7. How have total fertility rates changed in Kenya, China and Iran?

    Kenya's TFR was down to 3.85 in 2016. China's fell from 6.1 to 1.75 in just 35 years, dropped to 1.5 in 2010 and was 1.62 in 2016. Iran's fell from 6.8 in 1980 to 1.7 in 2016, once the government allowed family planning.

  8. Why are TFRs falling almost everywhere?

    Mostly family planning, pushed by government and NGO programs. Some women also choose fewer children because of economic and social uncertainty.

  9. How are some governments fighting low birth rates, and has it worked?

    Sweden, Russia and other European countries offer long maternity leave and state-paid daycare; Japan runs campaigns urging men to do more housework. Only limited success.

  10. Why is world population still growing?

    World TFR was 2.43 in 2016, above replacement, and India, Indonesia, Bangladesh, Pakistan and Nigeria are still adding lots of people.

  11. Which measure shows whether a population can replace itself, and which shows the burden an aging population puts on workers?

    The total fertility rate (TFR) shows whether a population can replace itself: 2.1 is replacement without immigration. The old-age dependency ratio shows the burden on workers: people 65+ per 100 people ages 15–64 (Europe 29.9, heading to 47).

  12. Why can a population keep growing for decades after its total fertility rate falls?

    Because so many of its people are young. A huge group of future parents (about 48% of Niger's people are under 15, Fig. 2.15) keeps births high even if each parent has fewer children than the last generation. AP calls this population momentum.

  13. What is the total dependency ratio, and how does it relate to the child and old-age ratios?

    The total dependency ratio counts both kinds of dependents: children ages 0–14 plus people 65 and older, for every 100 people ages 15–64. It's the child dependency ratio plus the old-age ratio, so a very young country (Africa: 74 children per 100 working-age people) and an aging one (Europe: 29.9 people over 65 per 100, heading to 47) can both have a high total.

Population pyramids pp. 25–28

  1. What are population composition and a population pyramid?

    Population composition is a population's makeup by age, sex and traits like marital status and education. A population pyramid is a graph of its age and sex structure.

  2. How do you read a population pyramid?

    Males on the left, females on the right; age groups (usually 5 years) up the side, youngest at the bottom. Every pyramid starts at 0 but they don't all end at the same age: countries with higher life expectancy have higher numbers in the top bracket, so always check the top bracket.

  3. Why are they called pyramids?

    Early on most countries had the most children and fewer people at each older age, which made a pyramid shape.

  4. Describe a lower-income country's pyramid. Which countries fit?

    It has a wide base and a narrow top, like a pyramid or evergreen tree (AP: an expansive pyramid). Children ages 0–14 make up over 40% of the population; from ages 15–19 up, each age group is smaller than the one below; the top three age groups together are under 10%. Examples: Pakistan, Yemen, Guatemala, Cameroon and Laos.

  5. Describe a higher-income country's pyramid. Which countries fit?

    It looks like a slightly lopsided chimney (AP: a stationary pyramid), biggest in the middle, with the bulge moving up as the population ages and TFR falls. Examples: Italy, France and Sweden.

  6. What does an expansive (expanding) pyramid, with a wide base and narrow top, tell you about births, deaths, growth and income?

    It shows a high TFR, high infant mortality and short lives (the narrow top), so the population has fast growth. It's typical of lower-income countries in stage 2 of the demographic transition model. The biggest burden is children: a high child dependency ratio (Africa: 74 children per 100 working-age people).

  7. What does a stationary (chimney-shaped) pyramid, with straight sides and a bulge in the middle, tell you?

    It shows a low TFR (near or below 2.1) and long lives, so the population has slow or stable growth. It's typical of higher-income countries in stage 4 of the demographic transition model, like France, Italy and Sweden. The bulge moves up as people age, so the old-age dependency ratio rises.

  8. What does a constrictive (contracting) pyramid, narrower at the base than in the middle, tell you?

    It shows a very low TFR for a long time and an aging population with rising deaths, so the population shrinks. It fits stage 5 of the demographic transition model, like Japan. Fewer young taxpayers support more retirees; the fixes are raising TFR or allowing immigration. (The reading doesn't name this shape; AP calls it constrictive.)

  9. What does AP call the three pyramid shapes, and which stages of the demographic transition model go with each?

    Expansive (wide base) goes with stages 1–2, and its base starts to narrow in stage 3. Stationary (chimney) goes with stage 4. Constrictive (narrower at the base than the middle) goes with stage 5. A stationary pyramid is not the same as the stationary population level (SPL), which is a population size.

Religion and growth pp. 26–28

  1. Which religions oppose contraception, and what's the Nigeria example?

    The Roman Catholic Church and some conservative branches of Islam. Northern Nigeria, where conservative Islam is practiced, grows faster than the south.

  2. Does religion predict growth in the Catholic world?

    Not well. Some of the slowest-growing places are in the Catholic heartland; the teaching seems followed more closely farther from the Vatican. The Philippines grows fairly fast.

  3. What's the point of the Bordeaux field note?

    Just in from weeks in sub-Saharan Africa (flying from Dakar), de Blij noticed most people in Bordeaux were adults and asked where the children were: France and Europe have fewer children and are aging.

  4. How fast do Saudi Arabia and Indonesia grow, and how did Indonesia bring its growth down?

    Saudi Arabia (home of Mecca) grows about 2% a year. Indonesia started family planning in 1970 despite conservative objections, using coercion and inducement; growth fell to 1.6% (2000) and 1.1% (2016).

  5. Compare how well policies to lower births and policies to raise births have worked, using the reading.

    Lowering births worked with nationwide family planning (Iran's TFR 6.8 → 1.7; Indonesia, mixing coercion and inducement, down to 1.1%), though India's 1976 forced sterilization set off riots and was dropped. Raising births, with Sweden's and Russia's maternity leave and daycare, has had only limited success. AP: antinatalist vs. pronatalist.

The demographic transition pp. 28–31

  1. What is the demographic transition model, and what is it based on?

    A model saying birth and death rates change in predictable ways as a country develops economically. It's based on western Europe after the Industrial Revolution.

  2. What happens to birth rates, death rates and growth in stage 1 of the demographic transition model?

    In stage 1, birth rates are high and death rates are just as high and swing up and down, so growth is low and unpredictable. Disease, crop failures and war push deaths up. Every place spent most of history in this stage.

  3. What was the Black Death, how did it spread, and how many people did it kill?

    The Black Death was bubonic plague in the 1300s. It started in Crimea, followed trade routes to Sicily and moved north by contagious diffusion on rats, whose fleas spread it. It came in waves and killed ¼ to ½ of the population, worst in the more connected west. Britain's population fell from nearly 4 million to just over 2 million.

  4. What else held stage 1 populations down?

    Famine and destructive wars also held stage 1 populations down. A famine just before the plague weakened people, and famines in India and China in the 1700s and 1800s killed millions.

  5. When did stage 2 begin in Europe, what cut death rates, and how far did they fall?

    Stage 2 began in Europe around 1750, when better food, sanitation, soap and medicine cut deaths while births stayed high. Europe's death rate may have averaged about 35 per 1,000 people a year before 1750; Britain's was about 16 per 1,000 by 1850, and its population exploded.

  6. Which countries are in stage 2 today, and what helped them?

    Lower-income countries like Palestine and Afghanistan, helped by the spread of Western medicine, including childhood vaccinations.

  7. What happens to birth rates, death rates and growth in stage 3, and why did Britain's birth rate fall?

    In stage 3, birth rates fall quickly, deaths keep falling, and natural increase is low (growth continues, more slowly). Britain was in stage 3 from about 1870 through the world wars. Its birth rate fell because of new opportunities for women, later marriage, and fewer infant and child deaths.

  8. What do Brazil and Mali show about falling birth rates?

    Brazil (a stage 3, middle-income country): its birth rate fell with access to modern contraceptives, not later marriage. Mali: girls who go to school end up with just over half as many children as women with no schooling.

  9. What happens to birth rates, death rates and growth in stage 4, and which countries are in it?

    In stage 4, birth and death rates are both low, and growth is stable or slowing. Britain reached it after 1950, when contraception and legal abortion kept births low. The U.S. and UK are in stage 4 today.

  10. What happens to birth rates, death rates and natural increase in stage 5, and which countries are in it?

    In stage 5, birth rates are very low, death rates are rising and natural increase is falling. Japan and Russia are in stage 5.

  11. How fast is world population growing now, and when might growth stop?

    The world grows about 1.2% a year, which still adds over 80 million people a year. Many demographers predict zero population growth within about 50 years, a stationary population level (SPL).

  12. What stationary population levels (SPLs) did the World Bank predict in the late 1980s, and how did those predictions hold up?

    The World Bank predicted each country's population would level off at: U.S. 276 million (2035), Brazil 353 million (2070), Mexico 254 million (2075), China 1.4 billion (2090), India 1.6 billion (2150). These were unrealistic: China passed 1.2 billion in 1994 and India reached 1 billion in 1998. Newer reports predict China will level off at 1.45 billion in 2030 and India at 1.7 billion in 2060.

  13. A middle-income country's birth rate is falling fast while its death rate keeps dropping. What stage is it in, and what do its growth and pyramid look like?

    Stage 3, like Brazil. It's still growing, but more slowly (Brazil: 2.9% → 0.8%), and its pyramid is still expansive, with a base that's narrowing as fewer babies are born.

  14. Give two limits of the demographic transition model, using examples from the reading.

    It's based only on western Europe, yet today's stage 2 countries (Afghanistan) cut deaths partly with imported medicine and vaccines, not their own industrialization. Low growth can come from crisis, not development (Russia after 1991). It also leaves out migration and can't predict timing (the failed SPL forecasts).

What medical geographers study p. 32

  1. Besides where diseases occur, what do medical geographers study?

    Medical geographers also study who can get medical care, and how inequality and the social norms of a particular place affect how diseases spread.

  2. Why does the reading call access to medical care "a geographic matter"?

    Access to medical care is geographic because it depends on the absolute distance to a clinic or hospital and on the transportation infrastructure available to get there. Infrastructure can block access or make it easier.

    HIV/AIDS transmission depends partly on attitudes toward using condoms, mobility patterns (how people move around), socioeconomic status, and other social conditions that differ from place to place.

    Chronic obesity is reaching epidemic levels where people have taken to heavily processed foods, or where those foods are the cheapest and easiest to find. Obesity goes along with more heart disease and more diabetes.

Infant mortality pp. 34–36

  1. What is the infant mortality rate (IMR), and in what unit is it given?

    The infant mortality rate is the chance that a baby dies before its first birthday, given as deaths per 1,000 live births.

  2. What is the child mortality rate (CMR), and in what unit is it given?

    The child mortality rate is the chance that a child dies before age 5, given as deaths per 1,000 live births (p. 34). Page 38 describes it as deaths between ages 1 and 5.

  3. What does the newborn death rate measure?

    The newborn death rate is the number of babies who die in their first month of life for every 1,000 live births.

  4. What typically causes newborn deaths in the United States and other higher-income countries?

    In the United States and other higher-income countries, most newborn deaths involve babies born too early (premature) or too small (low birthweight), according to the State of the World's Mothers report.

  5. What causes half of newborn deaths in lower-income countries?

    Diarrhea and infections cause half of all newborn deaths (deaths before 1 month old) in lower-income countries.

  6. According to the reading, what do a country's infant and child mortality rates reflect?

    Infant and child mortality rates reflect the overall health of the whole society, not just the health of its babies.

  7. What does the reading name as the major causes of high infant mortality rates?

    The major causes are the mother's physical health, including her access to prenatal care, and her access to sanitation and hygiene. Where most women have many babies, women also tend to be poorly fed and poorly educated.

  8. Which two causes kill the most infants and children around the world?

    Diarrhea and malnutrition kill more infants and children around the world than any other causes.

  9. How does the family tradition of women eating last raise infant deaths in lower-income countries?

    When women, even pregnant women, eat last, a mother may eat too little for her baby to reach a healthy birth weight, or to make nourishing breast milk after the birth.

  10. Why might a pregnant woman with little schooling ignore a health worker's advice about hand washing?

    A pregnant woman with little schooling may follow the advice she gets at home from her mother-in-law instead of the medical staff's advice about sanitation and hand washing to prevent diarrhea.

  11. How many of the world's people lack clean drinking water, hygienic waste disposal, and basic sanitation?

    About 30% of the world's people can't easily get clean drinking water. 6 in 10 people have no hygienic way to dispose of human waste. Up to 4 billion people have no basic sanitation, such as toilets.

  12. Which large country has long had the world's lowest infant mortality rate, and what is its rate?

    Japan has the lowest infant mortality rate of any large country: 1.9 infant deaths per 1,000 live births, with a population of more than 126 million.

  13. What are the infant mortality rates and populations of Singapore and Sweden?

    Singapore has an infant mortality rate of 2.2 infant deaths per 1,000 live births and over 5.4 million people. Sweden has a rate of 2.3 per 1,000 live births and 9.6 million people.

  14. Which five countries still had infant mortality rates of 70 or more deaths per 1,000 live births in 2017?

    The five countries were the Central African Republic, Sierra Leone, Somalia, Chad and the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

  15. Which country had the world's highest infant mortality rate in 2017, and how does the book describe it?

    The Central African Republic had the highest rate: 87.6 infant deaths per 1,000 live births, which the book describes as one or more deaths among every eight newborns.

  16. How many countries had infant mortality rates of 70 or more per 1,000 live births in 2012, and what is the global trend?

    In 2012, 11 countries had infant mortality rates of 70 or more, compared with five in 2017. Infant mortality has been declining worldwide, even in low-income regions.

  17. What pattern appears when you compare the infant mortality map (Fig. 2.21) with the crude birth rate map (Fig. 2.7)?

    High infant mortality rates and high crude birth rates occur in the same places, so the two are clearly correlated, as the Fig. 2.21 caption says.

  18. What can differences in infant mortality rates inside one country reveal?

    Differences inside a country show which groups (by region, ethnicity, social class or other lines) can reach health care and learn about health. A national rate is only an average.

  19. What is South Africa's national infant mortality rate, and how do the rates of its population groups compare?

    South Africa's infant mortality rate is 48 deaths per 1,000 live births. White South Africans' rate is near the European average, black Africans' is nearer the African average, and the "coloured" (multiracial) and Asian groups fall in between.

  20. Which three things make infant mortality rates differ from place to place?

    Infant mortality rates rise and fall with access to health care, sanitation and education.

    HIV/AIDS broke out in Africa about four decades ago and has killed about 35 million people worldwide.

  5. What is a vectored infectious disease, and what is the reading's main example?

Malaria and other vectored diseases pp. 41–43

  1. How does a mosquito pass malaria from one person to another, and what is a "host"?

    A mosquito bites an already infected person or animal (the host) and takes in parasites with the blood. The parasites multiply in the mosquito and reach its saliva, and its next bite injects them into a new person, who then becomes a host too.

  2. What are the symptoms of malaria, and what happens to people who survive it?

    Malaria causes recurring fever and chills, anemia and an enlarged spleen. Survivors gain some immunity, but many are left weak and low on energy, with a higher risk of catching other diseases.

  3. Which age group makes up most of malaria's victims, and what does that do to infant and child mortality?

    Most malaria victims are children 5 years old or younger, so malaria is a major cause of high infant and child mortality.

  4. Where does malaria not occur, which region suffers from it most, and where else is it common (Fig. 2.24)?

    Malaria does not occur at higher latitudes, at higher elevations or in drier places. Tropical Africa suffers most, and it is also common in the tropical Americas and parts of India and Southeast Asia.

  5. What record does malaria hold among all diseases in human history?

    No disease in human history has killed more people than malaria.

  6. Why are malaria infection rates falling in sub-Saharan Africa?

    More people are sleeping under insecticide-treated mosquito nets, which protect them from malaria mosquitoes that are most active at night.

  7. How are scientists hoping to use genetically engineered mosquitoes against malaria?

    They want to release mosquitoes engineered so they cannot pass on the malaria parasite, hoping the change spreads through the offspring of today's mosquitoes.

  8. How long does the yellow fever vaccine protect a person, and where is yellow fever still endemic?

    The yellow fever vaccine can give immunity for 10 years. The disease is still common, and has been endemic for a long time, in tropical Africa and South America.

  9. Besides malaria, which two diseases does the reading say are carried by mosquitoes?

    Mosquitoes also carry yellow fever and dengue.

  10. Besides mosquitoes, which four kinds of vectors does the reading name, and which three diseases do they carry?

    Fleas, flies, worms and snails carry diseases such as river blindness, guinea worm and elephantiasis.

  11. What carries sleeping sickness, and where and when did the disease begin?

    The tsetse fly carries sleeping sickness. It began around 1400 in West Africa and over the next few centuries reached much of sub-Saharan Africa.

  12. What does sleeping sickness do to people and animals, and how is it being fought?

    Sleeping sickness brings fever, then swollen lymph nodes and an inflamed brain, and it often kills. Campaigns to eradicate the tsetse fly have helped, but the disease still reaches large parts of sub-Saharan Africa.

  13. Why are tropical areas hit hardest by vectored diseases, and are these diseases found only in the tropics?

    Warm, moist tropical climates let vectors such as mosquitoes thrive, so the tropics are hit hardest. But vectored diseases are found around the world, not only in the tropics.

    The HIV/AIDS pandemic began in Africa, and Africa is also where its impact has been greatest; most cases are in sub-Saharan Africa.

    The worldwide infection rate has fallen dramatically since 2001 and keeps slowing, but HIV/AIDS has recently surged in eastern Europe and Central Asia.

    There were nearly 1 million AIDS deaths worldwide in 2017, according to the United Nations AIDS program.

    Countries that adopt restrictive policies usually have an expansive pyramid with a wide base and fast growth. China, for example, grew 3% a year in the 1970s, just before its one-child policy began in 1979.

One country, every measure 2.1–2.4

  1. Which kind of population policy goes with the demographic transition's stage 2, and which goes with stages 4 and 5?
